# Children’s > Action & Adventure Market Analysis

**U.S.-focused publishing outlook — August 2026**

## Executive Summary

Children’s action and adventure is a **durable but challenging market**, particularly in the middle-grade space.

The good news is that children still respond extremely well to adventure stories. Survival, secret missions, monsters, mysteries, magical quests, spies, dangerous expeditions, fantasy creatures and kids being forced to solve problems adults cannot are all commercially viable.

The harder news is that **middle-grade publishing has been soft for several years**. Circana reported middle-grade print sales down 5% in the first half of 2024, and industry professionals continued describing the category as difficult in 2025–26. Children's fiction overall slipped 0.8% in the first half of 2025. Publishers are therefore becoming more selective. ([Circana][1])

That does **not** mean publishers don't want adventure.

It means they increasingly want:

> **Shorter + faster + easier to understand + highly visual + immediately hooky + series-friendly.**

At the 2026 Bologna Children's Book Fair, agents repeatedly reported renewed signs of life in middle grade—but emphasized **commercial concepts, illustrated formats, short books, series and high-concept storytelling**. Writers House reported that some of its biggest current middle-grade sellers are humorous illustrated series of **30,000 words or less**. ([PublishersWeekly.com][2])

Meanwhile, recent acquisitions include:

* a child crossing the Moon to rescue his parents,
* kid monster hunters,
* covert child spies fighting monsters,
* dragon-egg smugglers,
* magical folklore adventures,
* museum treasure hunts,
* steampunk inventors,
* survival adventures,
* and action-heavy early chapter-book series. ([PublishersWeekly.com][3])

So the appetite is there.

My basic recommendation would be:

> ### **Don't write a children's adventure that's simply a smaller adult adventure.**
>
> Write a story that fulfills the childhood fantasy of **being capable, important and independent in a world that normally tells kids what to do.**

That is the heart of this category.

For what most people picture when they say:

### **Children's Action & Adventure novel**

## **Ages 8–12 / Middle Grade**

That's the commercial center I would build around.

# 2. The Current Middle-Grade Problem

This is worth understanding if you're approaching the category commercially.

Middle grade isn't currently enjoying the explosive demand of romantasy or adult romance.

Circana has repeatedly identified a decline in recreational reading among middle-grade children. In one study, only **14% of 13-year-olds reported reading for fun almost every day**, compared with 30% in 2004. Circana also found that middle-grade readers are particularly dependent on **friends and family for discovery**, rather than having something equivalent to adult BookTok. ([Circana][4])

That changes publishing strategy.

An adult reader can discover a novel on TikTok and click "Buy."

An eight-year-old usually needs some combination of:

**child wants book**

* **parent approves book**

* **parent buys book**

or:

**teacher/librarian recommends book**

* **child checks it out.**

You therefore have **multiple audiences**.

---

# 3. The Three Audiences You Are Actually Writing For

This is one of the biggest differences between children's publishing and adult publishing.

## Audience #1: The Child

They want:

> **"This looks awesome."**

They care about:

adventure, humor, danger, mystery, monsters, cool powers, friendship, exciting covers and characters they relate to.

---

## Audience #2: The Parent

They want:

> **"My kid will actually read this."**

And often:

> **"I'm comfortable with my kid reading this."**

Parents care about:

age appropriateness, readability, value, price, educational benefit, themes and whether the book might encourage continued reading.

---

## Audience #3: Teachers, Librarians and Booksellers

They want:

> **"I know exactly which kid to hand this book to."**

They care about:

reading level, age suitability, themes, representation, classroom usefulness, series availability, awards/reviews and broad child appeal.

The best children's adventure satisfies all three.

---

# 4. What Kids Want From Adventure

At its most fundamental, children's adventure provides a particular fantasy:

# **Agency.**

Children spend much of real life hearing:

Go to school.

Go to bed.

Do your homework.

Ask permission.

You aren't old enough.

Don't go there.

Let the adults handle it.

Adventure fiction flips that structure.

Suddenly:

### **The kid is the person who matters.**

The adults don't understand.

The parents are missing.

The police don't believe them.

The teacher doesn't know.

The map chose them.

The dragon needs them.

The aliens contacted them.

The secret organization recruits them.

The museum closes—and they're trapped inside.

Now the child gets to make decisions.

That's the fantasy.

---

# 5. Children Should Drive the Plot

This is arguably the most important rule in children's adventure.

The child protagonist should not merely follow adults around while the adults solve the problem.

Bad structure:

> Dad is an archaeologist. Twelve-year-old Ben goes on Dad's expedition and watches him discover Atlantis.

Better:

> **Dad disappears while searching for Atlantis, leaving Ben with the notebook containing the only clue to where he went.**

Now Ben owns the story.

Adults can:

help,

mentor,

protect,

teach,

cause problems,

or even rescue the protagonist occasionally.

But the protagonist needs to make the **decisive choices**.

---

# 6. Why Parents Keep Disappearing in Children's Adventure

There's a structural reason so many children's books contain:

orphans,

boarding schools,

summer camps,

absent parents,

missing parents,

busy parents,

single parents working long hours,

grandparents,

or children separated from adults.

A responsible parent would ruin most adventure plots.

If eleven-year-old Maya tells her mother:

> "There's a monster living under the school."

a competent parent says:

> "You're staying home while I call someone."

Story over.

You don't necessarily need to kill the parents.

You simply need to create a believable reason **the kids must act themselves.**

Good alternatives include:

**Parents don't believe them.**

**Parents are trapped too.**

**Adults literally cannot perceive the magical threat.**

**The child fears punishment if they reveal what happened.**

**The adventure occurs during camp/school/travel.**

**The parent is the person who needs rescuing.**

That last one is especially powerful.

# 7. Recent Acquisitions Show Exactly What Publishers Want

Look at several actual 2026 middle-grade deals.

### Space survival

A forthcoming middle-grade adventure pitched in the vein of *The Martian* follows **the first kid living in space**, whose parents don't return from a mission, forcing him to cross the Moon to save them. ([PublishersWeekly.com][3])

That's:

**child agency + survival + space + missing parents + rescue mission.**

---

### Monster spies

Penguin Workshop acquired a three-book middle-grade supernatural adventure series about an **11-year-old recruited into a covert kid-spy agency that battles monsters**. ([PublishersWeekly.com][5])

That's:

**secret organization + kid spies + monsters + series.**

---

### Dragon smuggling

Scholastic acquired *The Last Dragon Empire*, in which a **12-year-old takes over his father's dangerous job smuggling dragon eggs away from a prince's army**. ([PublishersWeekly.com][5])

Again:

**kid responsibility + dragons + dangerous mission + moral purpose.**

---

### Art-heist mystery

Atheneum acquired an adventure about **the daughter of an art thief and the son of the detective who arrested him**, who team up to uncover a hidden treasure at Chicago's Field Museum. ([PublishersWeekly.com][6])

That's:

**rivals + mystery + museum + treasure + family conflict.**

The common denominator isn't fantasy.

It's:

# **A kid gets an extraordinary job to do.**

---

# 8. The Best Adventure Hooks

For this genre, I strongly prefer premises that a child can explain to another child in one sentence.

For example:

> "It's about kids who discover their teacher is a spy."

> "It's about a girl who smuggles baby dragons."

> "It's about a boy trapped alone on the Moon."

> "It's about three kids who hunt monsters after school."

> "It's about a school hidden inside a volcano."

That clarity is enormously valuable.

Children's books do not benefit from complicated pitches.

# 9. Strong Action & Adventure Subgenres

## Adventure + Fantasy

Probably the strongest combination.

Examples:

dragons,

monsters,

magical kingdoms,

quests,

mythological creatures,

secret worlds,

**Demand:** ★★★★★

---

## Adventure + Mystery

Extremely strong.

Treasure hunts.

Codes.

Missing people.

Secret rooms.

Museum mysteries.

Lost artifacts.

Hidden societies.

**Demand:** ★★★★★

The mystery gives the adventure direction.

---

## Adventure + Humor

Especially important in the current market.

Industry professionals at Bologna 2026 specifically cited strong demand for **short, accessible, funny, illustrated middle-grade series**. ([PublishersWeekly.com][2])

Humor reduces the emotional intensity of danger.

A monster trying to eat you is scary.

A monster trying to eat you while arguing that you don't technically qualify as lunch because it's Tuesday becomes fun.

---

## Adventure + Survival

One of the most evergreen combinations.

Examples:

plane crash,

wilderness,

island,

shipwreck,

**Demand:** ★★★★☆

**Longevity:** ★★★★★

---

## Adventure + Science Fiction

Promising.

Especially:

space,

robots,

aliens,

Mars,

**Demand:** ★★★★☆

**Competition:** ★★★☆☆

**Opportunity:** ★★★★★

## Adventure + Horror/Monsters

Very strong with upper middle grade.

Think:

**Goosebumps energy rather than adult horror.**

Publishers continue to report interest in horror for upper middle-grade readers, particularly accessible versions rather than extreme gore. ([PublishersWeekly.com][2])

**Demand:** ★★★★☆

---

## Adventure + Historical

Still viable, especially when history creates the adventure rather than becoming a lesson.

Pirates.

Explorers.

War.

Shipwreck.

Archaeology.

Ancient civilizations.

Historical disasters.

The danger is writing:

> "Here's history disguised as homework."

# 11. The Trope Stack Works Here Too

Don't pitch:

> A children's adventure about two friends.

Pitch:

> **Two twelve-year-old rivals accidentally steal a pirate's enchanted compass and have 48 hours to return it before their landlocked Colorado town sinks beneath an invisible ocean.**

Now we have:

**rivals**

* **pirates**

* **magical artifact**

* **countdown**

* **quest**

* **hometown danger**

* **weird fantasy hook**

That's marketable.

# 12. Humor Is More Important Than Many Adult Writers Expect

If I were moving from adult fiction into middle grade, this is one adjustment I'd make immediately.

### Children often tolerate enormous danger if the tone remains fun.

A child can face:

dragons,

monsters,

death traps,

evil sorcerers,

aliens,

villains,

shipwrecks,

or kidnappers.

But humor provides pressure relief.

This is one reason franchises like:

**Diary of a Wimpy Kid**

**Dog Man**

**The Bad Guys**

and many heavily illustrated adventure series have such reach.

The 2025 children's bestseller lists were heavily dominated by established, visually distinctive franchises including *Dog Man*, *Diary of a Wimpy Kid*, *Wings of Fire*, and *Warriors*. ([PublishersWeekly.com][7])

The lesson isn't that every adventure must be comedy.

It's:

> ### Give children fun along with the danger.

---

# 13. The Villain

Children's villains can be genuinely threatening.

Do not assume the villain must be harmless.

Some of the most memorable children's villains are terrifying.

But I'd emphasize:

### **clarity of threat over graphic brutality.**

The villain can want to:

take over the school,

steal an artifact,

destroy the town,

capture dragons,

erase people's memories,

open a portal,

take control of the kingdom,

destroy the forest.

The danger is clear.

You don't need:

graphic torture,

prolonged gore,

sexual violence,

or adult-level sadism.

---

# 14. How Much Violence?

Middle-grade fiction can contain:

fights,

swords,

guns in appropriate contexts,

monsters,

death,

war,

danger,

injury,

and even significant character deaths.

What generally changes is **presentation**.

Instead of dwelling on anatomical injury:

> The sword split his abdomen and—

you'd more likely write:

> He fell and didn't get back up.

The emotional impact can still be enormous.

The rule isn't:

> Nothing bad can happen.

It's:

> **Don't luxuriate in suffering.**

---

# 15. Can Characters Die?

Absolutely.

Children can handle death.

But the question is:

### What purpose does it serve?

Death can establish stakes.

It can motivate the protagonist.

It can explore grief.

It can create sacrifice.

But repeated brutal deaths can push the manuscript toward YA or horror.

For core 8–12 adventure, I'd generally keep the **emotional world ultimately hopeful**, even if bad things happen.

---

# 16. Romance

For core middle grade:

### Keep it light.

Appropriate territory includes:

**crushes**

**embarrassment**

**first attraction**

**hand-holding**

**maybe a kiss**

But romance should generally not become the main engine.

The central relationships tend to be:

**friendship**

**siblings**

**parents**

**mentors**

**team dynamics**

That is a major distinction between middle grade and YA.

---

# 17. Friendship Is the Middle-Grade Love Story

This is one of the most useful ways to think about it.

In an adult romance:

> Will they fall in love?

In middle grade:

> **Will these kids remain friends?**

Friendship betrayal can feel catastrophic at this age.

You can generate enormous emotional stakes from:

jealousy,

secrets,

new friends,

rivalry,

feeling left behind,

betrayal,

social embarrassment,

or fear that growing up means growing apart.

The external adventure should often **test the friendship**.

---

# 18. Protagonist Age

My recommendation:

### Core 8–12 reader:

**Protagonist: 11–12**

This works extremely well because children tend to enjoy reading slightly "up."

An eight-year-old may happily read about an eleven-year-old.

An eleven-year-old may resist reading about a seven-year-old because it feels like a "baby book."

For upper-middle-grade adventure:

### **12–13**

can work well.

Once your protagonist reaches 14–15, editors may start questioning whether the manuscript belongs in YA.

---

# 19. Protagonists Should Have Flaws Children Understand

Avoid giving an eleven-year-old a primarily adult internal struggle such as:

> existential anxiety regarding professional fulfillment.

Good middle-grade struggles include:

**I'm afraid.**

**Nobody listens to me.**

**I don't fit in.**

**I'm jealous of my friend.**

**My family is changing.**

**I hate moving.**

**I'm embarrassed by my parents.**

**I want to prove myself.**

**I'm afraid I'll fail.**

**My brother gets all the attention.**

**Everyone thinks I'm weird.**

Then the adventure becomes a mechanism for confronting that emotional issue.

---

# 20. Adults Should Not Be Idiots

There's a temptation in children's adventure to make every adult incompetent.

I wouldn't.

Children notice when adults behave absurdly just so kids can solve the plot.

Better:

### Adults have different information.

The detective doesn't believe in ghosts.

Reasonable.

The scientist assumes the phenomenon has a natural explanation.

Reasonable.

The parent believes the child is scared after moving to a new home.

Reasonable.

Then the child discovers something adults couldn't reasonably know.

Now child agency doesn't require adult stupidity.

---

# 21. Pacing

This genre needs to move.

Especially now.

Scholastic is launching its **NEXT** middle-grade line in fall 2026 specifically around:

**fast plots**

**cliffhanger endings**

**shorter page counts**

**frequent illustrations**

and a serialized reading experience designed to compete with digital entertainment. ([PublishersWeekly.com][8])

That's an important market signal.

I would structure a commercial middle-grade adventure around short narrative cycles:

> **Goal → obstacle → solution → complication.**

Again.

> **Goal → obstacle → solution → complication.**

Chapters should frequently end with:

a discovery,

a joke,

a problem,

a question,

a threat,

or a decision.

Not necessarily:

> "AND THEN A MONSTER JUMPED OUT!"

every time.

But give the child a reason to say:

### "One more chapter."

---

# 22. Chapter Length

For modern commercial middle grade, I would generally favor:

### **1,000–2,000 words per chapter**

with considerable flexibility.

For younger/illustrated adventure:

### **500–1,500**

can work beautifully.

The psychological benefit is enormous.

A struggling reader sees:

> Chapter 7 — 5 pages.

and thinks:

> I can do that.

---

# 23. Word Count

This is one area where I would pay close attention to the current marketplace.

Historically, middle-grade adventure has often been around 35–60K.

But the current trend is toward accessibility.

At Bologna 2026, Writers House reported that its biggest-selling current middle-grade projects were often **30K or under**, particularly short, humorous illustrated series. ([PublishersWeekly.com][2])

So I would currently target:

| Type                          |     Target |
| ----------------------------- | ---------: |
| Early chapter adventure       |      5–12K |
| Illustrated chapter adventure |     10–25K |
| Younger MG adventure          | **20–35K** |
| Core MG adventure             | **30–50K** |
| Fantasy MG adventure          | **40–60K** |
| Upper MG adventure            | **45–65K** |

For a new commercially minded project:

# **35,000–45,000 words**

would be a very attractive target to me.

Short enough not to intimidate.

Long enough to deliver a substantial adventure.

---

# 24. Illustrations Are Becoming More Important

This is one of the strongest 2026 children's-publishing trends.

At Bologna, multiple agents and rights professionals independently reported growing demand for:

**graphic novels**

**heavily illustrated books**

**doodle-style illustrations**

**visual storytelling**

and books that ease children from picture books into longer prose. ([PublishersWeekly.com][2])

That doesn't mean every 10-year-old novel needs 100 pictures.

But illustrations can now be a **market advantage**, not evidence that the book is for very young children.

For a 30–40K adventure, I'd seriously consider:

**chapter-opening illustrations**

**maps**

**diagrams**

**notes**

**letters**

**fake documents**

**monster sketches**

**newspaper clippings**

**field guides**

**occasional full-page art**

These make a book feel interactive and accessible.

---

# 25. Graphic Novels Are Major Competition

This is critical.

Traditional prose children's books aren't competing only against:

video games,

YouTube,

TikTok,

TV.

They're also competing against:

### **graphic novels and manga.**

The 2025 children's bestseller list was full of graphic adaptations and visually driven franchises. Agents in 2026 continue to describe graphic novels as one of the strongest parts of middle grade. ([PublishersWeekly.com][7])

So prose adventure has to offer the same:

**speed**

**clarity**

**fun**

**strong visual imagination**

without necessarily becoming a graphic novel.

---

# 26. Series Are Extremely Valuable

I would strongly favor series potential.

One of the clearest signals from Bologna 2026 was international publisher interest in:

### **duologies, trilogies and continuing middle-grade series rather than standalones.** ([PublishersWeekly.com][2])

Recent deals reflect that.

Scholastic bought a middle-grade dragon series.

Penguin bought a three-book monster-spy series.

Several chapter-book deals involve multiple books from the beginning. ([PublishersWeekly.com][3])

Children particularly like recognizable worlds.

Book 1:

> We discover the Monster Bureau.

Book 2:

> The Monster Bureau investigates sea monsters.

Book 3:

> The Monster Bureau goes underground.

You don't have to re-sell the concept every time.

---

# 27. The Ideal Series Model

I'd favor:

### Same protagonist/team + new adventure each book + gradually expanding mythology.

For example:

**Book 1:** Find the missing dragon.

**Book 2:** Discover who is stealing dragon eggs.

**Book 3:** Stop the dragon hunters.

Each has:

**beginning**

**middle**

**ending**

while a larger story develops.

That's better for younger readers than making Book 1 feel like the first 300 pages of a 1,200-page story.

---

# 28. Standalones Are Harder

They're not impossible.

But agents reported in 2026 that middle-grade standalones remain **more difficult internationally**, although exceptional high-concept projects can break through. ([PublishersWeekly.com][2])

If I were pitching a standalone adventure, I'd want:

### an extraordinarily clean hook.

Something like:

> **A twelve-year-old has one night to escape a museum whose exhibits come alive after the last visitor leaves.**

That sounds complete.

And marketable.

---

# 29. Format Matters More Than in Adult Fiction

Children's books are physical objects.

Consider:

**trim size**

**font size**

**chapter length**

**white space**

**illustrations**

**map**

**page count**

**paperback price**

**series numbering**

**cover readability**

A 45K-word adult thriller might be extremely short.

A 45K-word illustrated children's hardcover can feel like a substantial novel.

Design changes how intimidating the book appears.

That's important.

---

# 30. Cover Expectations

The cover needs to answer:

### "What cool thing happens in this book?"

not merely:

### "This is literature for children."

Adventure covers usually benefit from:

**characters in motion**

**monster/creature**

**dangerous environment**

**large central artifact**

**strong title typography**

**high contrast**

**clear facial expression**

**visible stakes**

For example:

Kid hanging from rope over canyon while dragon approaches?

Adventure.

Kid standing thoughtfully beside abstract watercolor?

Probably not enough.

---

# 31. Titles

Children's adventure titles benefit tremendously from clarity and memorability.

Strong structures include:

### Character + extraordinary thing

**Percy Jackson and the Lightning Thief**

### The mysterious object/place

**The Mysterious Benedict Society**

### Action

**Escape from...**

**Race to...**

**The Hunt for...**

### Organization

**The Monster Hunters**

**The Secret Explorers**

**Kid Spy**

### Big impossible premise

**The Last Kids on Earth**

I would favor titles children can easily:

**remember**

**say**

**spell**

and

**tell their friend about.**

---

# 32. Diversity and Cultural Specificity

This can substantially improve an adventure if it's integrated into the concept.

Recent acquisitions include:

**Mexican folklore**

**Brazilian monsters**

**Victorian India**

**Korean mythology/dragons**

and culturally specific historical/fantastical settings. ([PublishersWeekly.com][9])

The strongest approach isn't:

> "Adventure story, but protagonist happens to be X."

It's:

> **What stories, monsters, histories, places or traditions let me create an adventure that wouldn't feel exactly the same with another setting?**

Specificity creates novelty.

---

# 33. Boys and Reading

This is one place where children's action/adventure can have strategic value.

The middle-grade market is trying to keep **reluctant readers** engaged, including children who migrate heavily toward games, manga and other visually driven entertainment.

Fast-moving adventure can serve this audience particularly well.

I would therefore take seriously:

**short chapters**

**maps**

**illustrations**

**clear goals**

**humor**

**action**

**accessible prose**

**series branding**

But I would avoid declaring:

> "This is a boys' book."

Girls read action.

Boys read friendship.

The better strategy is **broad appeal**.

---

# 34. Reading Level

A middle-grade adventure can contain sophisticated ideas while using straightforward language.

For core MG, I'd target prose that generally feels comfortable around:

### **4th–6th grade reading fluency**

depending on the exact age and format.

That doesn't mean writing stupidly.

Compare:

> The creature's anomalous anatomical morphology created significant consternation among the assembled children.

with:

> The creature had six legs.

> Nobody moved.

> Then it smiled.

The second is stronger writing anyway.

---

# 35. Don't Preach

Children are extremely sensitive to being lectured.

You can write about:

climate,

racism,

bullying,

family,

grief,

immigration,

poverty,

disability,

environmental destruction,

war,

or prejudice.

But make those things **part of the story**.

Don't stop the adventure so the protagonist can deliver the lesson.

If the theme is courage:

### Make the child have to be courageous.

You don't need three paragraphs explaining courage.

---

# 36. Educational Value Can Be a Bonus

This genre can stealthily teach.

Adventure works particularly well with:

**science**

**history**

**geography**

**mythology**

**animals**

**space**

**archaeology**

**survival skills**

**coding**

**engineering**

But the hierarchy should be:

# **Story first. Learning second.**

If a kid closes the book thinking:

> "I didn't know Moon dust was like that."

fantastic.

If they close it thinking:

> "That was homework."

you lost.

# 38. Concepts I'd Consider Crowded

Not dead—but requiring differentiation.

### Generic magic school

You need a new mechanism.

### Generic kid discovers he's secretly magical

Very familiar.

### Generic mythology descendant

"You're the son/daughter of a god" needs another hook.

### Generic dragon rider

Still popular, heavily used.

### Generic chosen one

Particularly if adults simply tell the child they're special.

### Generic kids save fantasy kingdom

What makes this kingdom/problem distinctive?

### Generic treasure hunt

What is the treasure, and why is finding it uniquely dangerous?

### Generic kid spy

Needs a distinctive agency or mission.

### Generic portal fantasy

Finding another world isn't enough.

Ask:

> What is wrong with that world?

# 40. Traditional Publishing vs. Self-Publishing

This is one genre where I lean significantly toward:

# **Traditional publishing.**

Why?

Because children's books depend heavily on:

**libraries**

**schools**

**book fairs**

**bookstores**

**professional reviews**

**distribution**

**parents**

**teachers**

**illustrators**

and institutional discovery.

An adult indie romance author can reach the reader directly through Amazon/KU.

An indie children's author has to somehow reach an eight-year-old **through the adults controlling the purchase**.

That's much harder.

Traditional publishing also handles:

illustration,

school distribution,

library sales,

foreign rights,

and trade relationships.

# 44. My Preferred Commercial Formula

Of everything in the category, I'd particularly favor:

# **Adventure + mystery + fantastic/scientific hook + team of kids + humor.**

For example:

> **Twelve-year-old Milo spends the summer helping his grandmother clean abandoned storage units. When he finds a spacesuit inside one unit containing a map of the Moon and a photograph of himself standing there, Milo and his two best friends have three days to figure out where it came from before the men who abandoned it come looking for it.**

That gives me:

**ordinary kid**

* **mystery**

* **science fiction**

* **friend group**

* **danger**

* **countdown**

* **impossible discovery**

* **series potential.**

And most importantly:

A ten-year-old could tell another ten-year-old what the book is about.

# 45. The Biggest Difference From the Adult Genres We've Discussed

With adult genres, we have repeatedly talked about finding an interesting variation on a known reader fantasy.

Children's adventure works similarly, but the emotional fantasy is remarkably consistent:

### Contemporary romance:

**Someone chooses me.**

### Fantasy romance:

**I become powerful and someone extraordinary chooses me.**

### SF adventure:

**I discover the impossible.**

### Dark fantasy:

**I encounter something I shouldn't understand.**

### Children's action/adventure:

# **I get to do something adults think I'm too young to do.**

That's the engine.

The kid gets:

the sword,

the map,

the spaceship,

the monster,

the secret,

the mission,

the code,

the responsibility.

And then the child discovers:

> **Maybe I actually can do this.**

# Bottom Line

I like Children's > Action & Adventure as a creative category, but I would approach it **very differently from the adult categories we've discussed**.

The overall middle-grade marketplace remains difficult: reading-for-pleasure has declined, discovery is harder, international sales remain soft, and publishers are cautious. ([Circana][1])

But the industry's response to that problem is actually very clear.

Publishers want books that can **pull kids back toward reading**:

> **shorter, faster, funnier, more visual, more immediately understandable and easier to turn into a series.**

Scholastic's new NEXT program is essentially an institutional response to exactly that problem, using shorter books, cliffhangers, illustrations and serialized commercial storytelling to compete for children's attention. ([PublishersWeekly.com][8])

So if I were developing for this market, my formula would be:

# **11–12-year-old protagonist + irresistible one-sentence premise + clear mission + mystery + real danger + friendship + humor + short chapters + 35–45K words + visual elements + strong series engine.**

And of the niches available, I'd currently be most interested in **mystery-adventure, monster hunting, kid spies, unusual survival situations, science/space adventure, culturally specific folklore adventure, and kid-profession concepts** where the protagonist gets to do an extraordinary job normally reserved for adults.

Those align particularly well with the projects publishers are actually acquiring for 2027–28. ([PublishersWeekly.com][3])
