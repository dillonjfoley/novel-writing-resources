# Epic Fantasy

## Market & Publishing Analysis

**U.S.-focused publishing outlook — August 2026**

## Executive Summary

**Epic fantasy remains one of fiction's most durable genres, but its commercial environment has changed significantly.**

The audience for fantasy itself is large: **23% of Americans who read at least one book in 2025 said they read fantasy**, with the genre attracting similar shares of male and female readers.

But raw fantasy sales need context.

U.S. adult fantasy print sales:

* rose **35.8% in 2024**
* then fell **8.7% in 2025**, to about **24.1 million units**

That decline followed an extraordinary romantasy-driven 2024 and therefore doesn't mean readers suddenly abandoned fantasy.

The bigger issue for traditional epic fantasy is that **fantasy's center of commercial gravity has moved**.

In the U.K., science fiction/fantasy sales were running **16% above 2025 levels as of August 2026**, but **39 of the top 50 SFF books were romantasy**, and more than half of the scheduled autumn SFF hardcovers were romantasy titles.

So epic fantasy is not competing merely against other epic fantasy.

It is competing for:

**publisher slots**

**bookstore space**

**reader attention**

**special-edition spending**

and **social-media visibility**

with romantasy, cozy fantasy, progression fantasy, dark fantasy, LitRPG, and other increasingly specific fantasy niches.

The consequence is simple:

> ### "A sweeping fantasy epic set in a richly imagined world" is no longer a sufficient commercial pitch.

Nearly every epic fantasy claims that.

What matters now is:

> **What is this epic actually about?**

### Overall recommendation

**Epic fantasy remains viable, but it is one of the genres where concept discipline matters most.**

The strongest commercial formula today is:

> **Immediately understandable conflict + distinctive secondary world + memorable characters + clear magic/fantasy hook + escalating scope + series potential.**

Not:

> "Here are 6,000 years of my world's history."

---

# 1. What Exactly Is Epic Fantasy?

Epic fantasy usually combines several elements:

**Secondary world**

**Large-scale conflict**

**Multiple factions or cultures**

**Magic or supernatural forces**

**Political or military stakes**

**journeys/quests**

**civilization-level consequences**

**substantial worldbuilding**

and often:

**multiple protagonists.**

The simplest defining characteristic is probably:

# **Scale.**

The story ultimately concerns something larger than one household, one romance, or one murder.

Kingdoms fall.

Empires rise.

Religions fracture.

Armies invade.

Gods awaken.

Magic changes.

Civilizations face extinction.

---

# 2. Epic Fantasy vs. High Fantasy

These terms overlap, but they aren't identical.

### High Fantasy

Primarily refers to fantasy deeply embedded in an invented world with significant fantastical elements.

### Epic Fantasy

Primarily refers to **scope and stakes**.

You can therefore write:

**High fantasy that isn't especially epic**

such as a magical secondary-world story about one village.

And:

**Epic fantasy with relatively restrained magic**

such as enormous wars between invented nations.

Most classic epic fantasy is also high fantasy.

But the useful commercial question is:

> **Does the story feel enormous?**

---

# 3. Epic Fantasy vs. Romantasy

This distinction is extremely important right now.

### Romantasy

The reader is heavily invested in:

> **Will these characters get together?**

### Epic Fantasy

The central question is closer to:

> **What happens to these characters and their world?**

Romance can be important.

Characters can fall deeply in love.

Relationships can shape wars.

But if removing the central romance causes the entire novel to stop functioning, you may be closer to fantasy romance.

That matters because **reader expectations differ dramatically**.

Epic fantasy readers will accept:

betrayal,

tragic romance,

partners dying,

relationships ending,

and characters choosing duty over love.

Romance readers may not.

---

# 4. Epic Fantasy vs. Grimdark

Grimdark is primarily a tonal/philosophical lane.

It generally emphasizes:

**moral ambiguity**

**violence**

**cynicism**

**political brutality**

**compromised protagonists**

**institutions behaving badly**

Epic fantasy can be grimdark.

But it can also be:

**heroic**

**hopeful**

**mythic**

**romantic**

**adventurous**

**tragic**

**whimsical**

or

**spiritual.**

One thing I would *not* assume in 2026 is:

> Epic fantasy needs to be bleak to feel adult.

The genre has moved well beyond that debate.

---

# 5. Target Audience

Fantasy has one advantage over several genres we've discussed:

### Its readership is relatively broad by gender.

YouGov's 2025 reading survey found fantasy read by similar shares of men and women, unlike romance or mystery/crime, which have stronger gender skews.

Older YouGov data also found physical-book fantasy purchasing particularly strong among younger adults, with **37% of physical-book readers ages 18–29 saying they tended to purchase fantasy**, higher than older groups.

For commercial adult epic fantasy, I would therefore think primarily:

### Core

**Adults approximately 18–44**

with substantial readership beyond that.

I would not market epic fantasy as a "male genre."

That stereotype is increasingly outdated.

---

# 6. What Epic Fantasy Readers Want

At the deepest level, epic fantasy sells:

# **immersion.**

Readers want the feeling that:

> **There is an entire world beyond the edges of this page.**

They want:

cultures,

cities,

history,

religions,

wars,

magic,

mythology,

languages,

monsters,

politics,

legends,

and unexplored places.

But there is a trap here.

Readers want to **feel** that the world exists.

They do not necessarily want you to **explain all of it.**

That's an enormous difference.

---

# 7. The Great Epic Fantasy Mistake

Aspiring epic-fantasy writers frequently think:

> The more worldbuilding I put into the novel, the more immersive it becomes.

Not necessarily.

Immersion comes from:

**specificity**

rather than:

**volume of information.**

Compare:

> The Kingdom of Aranth was founded 1,142 years ago by King Torval the Great following the War of the Seven—

with:

> Nobody wore blue inside the palace. It had been the color of treason for eight hundred years.

The second tells me:

history exists,

culture exists,

politics exist,

and something awful happened.

Without stopping the story.

That's good worldbuilding.

---

# 8. Worldbuilding Should Create Problems

One of the strongest principles for modern commercial epic fantasy is:

### **Worldbuilding should generate plot.**

Don't just invent:

a religion.

Make two religions incompatible.

Don't just invent:

dragons.

Make the kingdom economically dependent on dragon migration.

Don't just invent:

magic.

Make control of magic determine political power.

Don't just invent:

seven kingdoms.

Give them a reason to hate each other.

Every major worldbuilding element should ideally create:

**opportunity**

or

**conflict.**

---

# 9. BookBub's Fantasy Data Gives an Interesting Signal

BookBub's August 2026 reader-response data has:

**"fantasy novel" at +36% above its broader SFF-category average**

and

**"dragon rider" at +14%.**

BookBub has also historically described epic fantasy as the **"bread and butter" of its Fantasy category**, identifying reluctant heroes, prominent magic, human-dragon relationships, and high-stakes quests as particularly effective hooks.

That tells me traditional fantasy appetites haven't disappeared.

But the phrase:

> **Epic fantasy**

by itself is not necessarily the selling point.

Readers still react to tangible things like:

**dragons**

**quests**

**magic**

**unlikely heroes**

**wars**

**monsters**

and recognizable fantasy situations.

---

# 10. The Best Epic Fantasy Pitches Are Surprisingly Simple

Epic fantasy books may be complicated.

Their pitches shouldn't be.

Bad:

> After centuries of warfare between the Alcarian Dominion and the descendants of the Seven Celestial Houses, competing magical orders—

I've already forgotten everything.

Better:

> **The emperor has died, and his five children must compete for the throne while an army they thought extinct crosses the northern border.**

Now I understand the story.

You can introduce the:

magic,

religions,

history,

ethnic conflict,

political structure,

and ancient gods

inside the novel.

The pitch sells the **conflict**.

---

# 11. The Trope Stack

Like the other commercial genres we've discussed, epic fantasy benefits from combining several recognizable hooks.

For example:

**succession crisis**

*

**dragon riders**

*

**religious schism**

*

**siblings at war**

becomes:

> **When the emperor dies without naming an heir, his five children divide the empire's remaining dragons between them—until the youngest discovers the gods have ordered her to destroy her own dynasty.**

Now there are multiple reader entry points.

That's much stronger than:

> A war threatens a magical empire.

# 13. The Chosen One

Still viable.

Extremely familiar.

The problem isn't prophecy.

It's predictability.

Classic:

> You are the chosen one who will defeat the Dark Lord.

More interesting:

> **Everyone knows who the chosen one is—and the protagonist has been ordered to assassinate him.**

Or:

> The prophecy has already failed.

Or:

> There are five people who satisfy the prophecy.

Or:

> The chosen one refuses.

Or:

> The prophecy was invented politically but is accidentally becoming true.

Use the trope.

Complicate the assumption.

---

# 14. The Dark Lord Problem

Generic Dark Lord villains are one of the hardest sells in modern adult fantasy.

> Evil sorcerer wants to conquer the world because evil.

That's thin.

A strong epic antagonist should usually believe:

### **They are fixing something.**

Maybe:

The empire really is collapsing.

Magic really is dangerous.

The gods really are returning.

The monarchy really is corrupt.

The rebellion really will cause chaos.

The antagonist's solution is simply unacceptable.

That's much more interesting.

---

# 15. But Don't Make Everyone Morally Gray

There has been such a long reaction against black-and-white fantasy that the opposite can become monotonous.

If:

every king is corrupt,

every priest is a hypocrite,

every hero is secretly terrible,

every rebel commits atrocities,

and nobody believes in anything,

the reader eventually loses emotional investment.

Moral complexity doesn't require moral emptiness.

You can have:

### genuinely good people confronted with impossible choices.

That is often more emotionally powerful.

---

# 16. Heroism May Actually Be an Opportunity

For years, adult epic fantasy heavily emphasized:

**deconstruction**

**antiheroes**

**grim realism**

**cynicism**

**subverted prophecy**

**failed institutions.**

All of that remains useful.

But I think there is considerable opportunity now for:

### **earnest heroism without naïveté.**

Characters who genuinely believe:

people are worth saving,

promises matter,

sacrifice matters,

and evil should be resisted—

while living in a complicated world.

There's room between:

> Tolkien imitation

and

> everyone is horrible.

---

# 17. Dragons Are Not Dead

Dragons remain commercially legible.

The enormous success of the *Empyrean* books has reinforced rather than destroyed reader interest in dragons, and BookBub's current "dragon rider" phrase is performing **14% above its broader SFF average**.

But:

### Dragon rider alone is not the premise.

I'd differentiate through function.

What are dragons **for**?

War?

Agriculture?

Religion?

Transportation?

Mining?

Diplomacy?

Execution?

Weather control?

Migration?

Medicine?

What happens to society because dragons exist?

That's epic-fantasy worldbuilding.

---

# 18. Magic Systems

Epic fantasy supports everything from:

### Soft magic

mysterious,

mythic,

unexplained,

wonder-driven

to:

### Hard magic

rules,

costs,

limitations,

repeatable mechanisms.

Modern epic fantasy readers generally tolerate either.

What matters is consistency with the story.

If the climax depends on clever magical problem-solving:

### The reader needs to understand the rules.

If magic represents gods, mystery, or cosmic forces:

### Leaving some uncertainty may be preferable.

---

# 19. The Best Magic Has Political Consequences

Don't stop with:

> Certain people can control fire.

Ask:

Who controls them?

Are they military property?

Do they rule?

Are they feared?

Can common people kill them?

Is magic hereditary?

Can it be purchased?

Who trains mages?

What happens if someone refuses?

How does magic affect:

**war**

**law**

**religion**

**class**

**medicine**

**trade**

**architecture**

**agriculture**

**crime?**

That's how magic becomes worldbuilding rather than combat choreography.

---

# 20. Religion Is Underused as a Genuine World Force

Many fantasy worlds have:

"the church"

or

"the gods"

without religion meaningfully affecting everyday life.

Epic fantasy has enormous room for:

**religious schisms**

**competing interpretations**

**saints**

**pilgrimage**

**ritual**

**sacred law**

**heresy**

**divine kingship**

**atheism in a world with gods**

**religious reform**

**false miracles**

**real miracles**

Religion can create conflict on the same scale as monarchy or magic.

Especially if:

### the gods actually exist.

---

# 21. Political Fantasy

This remains one of epic fantasy's strongest engines.

Readers tend to respond well to:

**succession**

**alliances**

**betrayal**

**diplomacy**

**marriage**

**rebellion**

**assassination**

**war councils**

**competing factions.**

But political complexity must remain emotionally comprehensible.

Readers don't need to remember:

42 noble houses.

They need to understand:

> **If she supports House A, her brother dies. If she supports House B, her country goes to war.**

That's politics as drama.

---

# 22. War

Epic fantasy naturally accommodates war.

But battles should change something.

After a battle:

someone dies.

A city falls.

An alliance collapses.

The hero loses credibility.

An army cannot recover.

The enemy gains ground.

The moral cost increases.

Battle scenes that exist merely because:

> epic fantasy needs battles

become repetitive quickly.

The reader should ask:

> **What does winning this battle cost?**

---

# 23. Military Fantasy Has Opportunity

I particularly like the combination:

### **Epic fantasy + military campaign**

when it's accessible to non-military readers.

Possible perspectives include:

general,

ordinary soldier,

battlefield healer,

quartermaster,

spy,

engineer,

priest,

civilian,

diplomat.

There's considerable room beyond:

> sword guy fights another sword guy.

Logistics can actually create fantastic stakes:

food,

winter,

disease,

terrain,

horses,

magic supply,

refugees,

communications.

---

# 24. Multi-POV Epic Fantasy

Very natural.

But this is an area where authors often overdo it.

I'd think approximately:

### 2–4 major POVs for a debut

as a particularly manageable range.

Once you're at:

10 viewpoint protagonists,

your book needs substantial length just to maintain emotional continuity.

Every POV should answer:

> **What can this character show us that nobody else can?**

Not:

> The world is big, so I need everyone.

---

# 25. Character Before Geography

A world map doesn't create emotional investment.

Characters do.

A reader doesn't care that:

Kingdom A invades Kingdom B

until:

someone they love lives in Kingdom B.

Epic scale only works when it is attached to individuals.

A useful structural progression is:

**Save myself**

→ **save my family**

→ **save my city**

→ **save my country**

→ **save the world.**

The reader's emotional investment expands alongside the stakes.

---

# 26. Found Family

Still extremely effective.

Epic fantasy naturally throws together characters from different:

cultures,

classes,

religions,

races,

nations,

and professions.

That's ideal for:

### **reluctant team → trust → loyalty → sacrifice.**

The party structure remains one of fantasy's most durable pleasures for a reason.

Wizard.

Knight.

Thief.

Prince.

Monster.

Scholar.

Different archetypes can clash while giving readers multiple characters to love.

---

# 27. The Quest Is Still Good

You don't need to apologize for using a quest.

BookBub has specifically identified **high-stakes quests** as a strong epic-fantasy reader hook.

The issue is:

> What makes this quest different?

Generic:

> Take magical object across continent.

Specific:

> **Carry the dead king's body across enemy territory before it decomposes, because the kingdom's law says only a king buried beneath the capital may name his successor.**

Same journey structure.

Different story.

---

# 28. Secondary Worlds Need Distinctive Identity

One of the biggest challenges is:

### Faux-medieval-Europe blur.

King.

Castle.

Village.

Knight.

Tavern.

Forest.

Blacksmith.

Gold coins.

Generic church.

Generic peasant.

None of these are wrong.

But together they can make your book feel like default fantasy settings loaded from a video-game menu.

You need some element that tells me:

> **This is your world.**

---

# 29. Distinctive Doesn't Mean Weird for Weird's Sake

You don't need:

purple oceans,

six moons,

seven invented pronouns,

and upside-down cities

just to be different.

Distinctive can come from one foundational premise.

For example:

> The world's major rivers are living gods.

Immediately that affects:

transportation,

religion,

war,

borders,

cities,

agriculture,

and politics.

One strong worldbuilding decision can do more than 100 minor inventions.

---

# 30. Non-European Inspirations

There remains substantial room for fantasy drawing from histories and cultures beyond generic medieval Western Europe.

But authenticity and specificity matter.

The goal shouldn't be:

> Medieval Europe, except I renamed castles.

Nor:

> Fantasy Japan.

Ask instead:

What political system?

What architectural logic?

What religious traditions?

What trade networks?

What family structures?

What historical pressures?

Specificity creates originality.

---

# 31. Industrial and Early-Modern Epic Fantasy

This is one direction I find particularly attractive.

Epic fantasy doesn't have to mean:

### 1200 AD forever.

Consider worlds undergoing:

**industrialization**

**printing**

**gunpowder**

**railroads**

**telegraph equivalents**

**mass politics**

**colonial collapse**

**urbanization**

**early capitalism**

**scientific revolution**

Then ask:

> What happens to magic when technology starts replacing it?

That gives you an epic conflict beyond:

"evil army invades."

---

# 32. Cross-Genre Epic Fantasy

One of the strongest strategies available is giving epic fantasy another genre engine.

### Epic fantasy + mystery

Who murdered the emperor?

### Epic fantasy + horror

Why are the dead soldiers returning?

### Epic fantasy + heist

Steal the artifact before the war begins.

### Epic fantasy + political thriller

Prevent the assassination that will trigger invasion.

### Epic fantasy + adventure

Reach the lost continent.

### Epic fantasy + military fiction

Survive the campaign.

### Epic fantasy + romance

Political/character romance without making the entire book romantasy.

This helps answer the crucial question:

> **What actually happens for 500 pages?**

# 33. Word Count

Epic fantasy is one of the few genres where readers readily accept long books.

Publishers are another matter.

For a traditionally published debut, I'd target:

| Type                    |             Target |
| ----------------------- | -----------------: |
| Compact epic fantasy    |            90–105K |
| Standard epic fantasy   |       **100–120K** |
| Multi-POV epic          |           110–130K |
| Established-author epic |              120K+ |
| Indie epic fantasy      | 100–160K+ can work |

For a debut seeking an agent, my preferred range would be:

# **100,000–115,000 words**

with perhaps **120K** still quite defensible.

Once you get toward:

150K,

170K,

200K,

you are increasing:

editing costs,

printing costs,

production complexity,

and perceived acquisition risk.

A long book isn't automatically more epic.

---

# 34. Series Length

Classic epic fantasy frequently became:

seven,

ten,

fourteen-book

series.

For a new traditionally published author, I would not pitch that as the required plan.

I'd favor:

### Trilogy

or increasingly:

### Duology.

A recent 2026 Saga acquisition, for example, bought debut fantasy *The Godbound King* as the first half of a two-book deal involving malevolent gods, religious power, a usurper king, and expansion into dangerous territory.

The safer pitch is:

> **Book 1 has a satisfying arc. The larger story can continue.**

Not:

> You need to commit to nine books before anything resolves.

---

# 35. Book 1 Must Be a Book

This matters tremendously.

Don't write:

# Book 1: Everybody meets.

# Book 2: The journey begins.

# Book 3: Something happens.

The first novel needs:

**its own problem**

**its own climax**

**its own emotional transformation**

even if the war continues afterward.

Series investment becomes easier when readers feel:

> I got a satisfying story and want more.

Not:

> I paid $30 for the prologue.

# 36. The Indie Market

Epic fantasy has real indie potential.

Fantasy represented **14% of respondents** in Written Word Media's 2025 indie-author survey, second only to romance among the major listed genres. The survey found fantasy among the genres with meaningful mid-tier and some high-income indie authors, particularly where writers build **series, substantial catalogs, and clearly targeted subgenres**.

That is important because epic fantasy naturally supports:

**series**

**boxed sets**

**omnibuses**

**special editions**

**direct sales**

**audiobooks**

and

**long-tail readership.**

BookBub has also reported especially strong Fantasy-category performance from complete-series box sets.

---

# 37. But Indie Epic Fantasy Has a Production Problem

Epic fantasy is expensive.

Readers often expect:

professional cover,

map,

editing,

large word count,

series continuity,

and preferably audio.

That's much more expensive than producing:

an 80K thriller with one protagonist.

The financial model improves once multiple books exist because:

> selling Book 1 can sell Books 2, 3, 4...

But the initial investment can be substantial.

---

# 38. Kindle Unlimited vs. Wide

There is no universal correct answer.

Written Word Media's 2025 survey found Amazon remained the largest revenue source for **83% of surveyed indie authors**. Among authors earning more than $5,000 per month, 43% had their entire catalog in KDP Select, though successful wide authors also existed.

Epic fantasy has advantages in KU because:

### long books generate substantial page reads.

But wide distribution can work well because fantasy audiences also use:

Kobo,

libraries,

direct stores,

international retailers,

and special editions.

I'd base the decision on the author's broader business strategy.

---

# 39. Audiobooks Are Extremely Important

Epic fantasy is one of the genres I would prioritize for audio.

U.S. audiobook revenue increased **9% in 2025 to $2.43 billion**, and science fiction/fantasy remains one of the industry's leading audiobook genres.

Epic fantasy fits audio particularly well because readers often:

**consume long series**

**reread earlier installments**

**listen during commutes/work**

and become attached to narrators.

A strong narrator can become part of the brand.

The downside:

A 150K-word fantasy audiobook is expensive to produce.

---

# 40. Progression Fantasy Is Changing Reader Expectations

This is technically an adjacent category rather than traditional epic fantasy.

But it matters.

Progression fantasy, LitRPG, cultivation fantasy, and web-serial-derived fantasy have trained a large portion of the audience to enjoy:

**rapid advancement**

**clear goals**

**constant progression**

**long series**

**frequent payoffs**

**highly readable prose.**

*Dungeon Crawler Carl*, an indie-origin LitRPG phenomenon, was the **#9 bestselling U.S. print book across all categories in Q1 2026**, selling more than 183,000 copies during the quarter.

Traditional epic fantasy doesn't need:

levels,

stats,

or skill trees.

But it can learn from the pacing principle:

### The protagonist should make visible progress.

---

# 41. Pacing

Epic fantasy can be slower than thrillers.

But slow doesn't mean static.

Something should continuously change:

**political position**

**knowledge**

**relationships**

**geography**

**military situation**

**magical capability**

**character belief.**

A 500-page novel cannot simply be:

> traveling and discussing world history.

The reader needs narrative milestones.

---

# 42. How Soon Should Something Happen?

Sooner than many epic-fantasy writers think.

You don't necessarily need:

battle on page 2.

But the destabilizing event should usually appear early.

The king dies.

The village burns.

The monster returns.

The prophecy fails.

The army crosses the border.

The protagonist is arrested.

The god speaks.

The magic stops working.

Then worldbuilding unfolds around the problem.

---

# 43. Prologues

Epic fantasy is infamous for:

### 3,000-year-old prologue involving characters the reader won't see again for 400 pages.

Sometimes it works.

Often it creates unnecessary distance.

My rule:

### Use a prologue only if the prologue itself is compelling.

Not because:

> The reader needs to understand the mythology.

If the mythology is necessary, the novel should be capable of teaching it organically.

---

# 44. Maps

Yes.

Epic fantasy readers still like maps.

Maps:

**increase immersion**

**help explain journeys**

**reinforce scale**

and

**look great in special editions.**

But:

### The map should support the story.

A map containing 83 labeled cities that never appear is decorative cartography rather than useful storytelling.

Nothing wrong with that for personal worldbuilding.

You don't need all of it in the book.

---

# 45. Covers

Epic fantasy packaging has historically relied on:

**landscapes**

**warriors**

**weapons**

**dragons**

**castles**

**crowns**

**maps**

**symbols**

**large serif typography.**

But the current fantasy marketplace creates a problem:

You need to avoid accidentally signaling:

### Romantasy

if romance isn't central.

A black cover with:

gold floral decoration + dagger + crown

may now communicate romantasy.

Traditional epic fantasy can differentiate with:

**large environments**

**battle imagery**

**creatures**

**strong heraldic symbols**

**maps**

**illustrated scenes**

**less romantic decorative language.**

The buyer should immediately understand:

> **This is a big fantasy adventure.**

# 46. Special Editions

Fantasy is exceptionally suited to premium editions.

Potential features include:

**sprayed edges**

**foil**

**maps**

**illustrated endpapers**

**character art**

**ribbons**

**slipcases**

# 51. Traditional Publishing

I'd rate the environment:

# **★★★☆☆ — viable but difficult.**

Publishers absolutely still acquire substantial secondary-world fantasies. For example, Saga acquired debut *The Godbound King* in a two-book deal in August 2026, built around malevolent gods, religious authority, territorial expansion, and a usurper king.

But romantasy currently occupies an enormous amount of fantasy acquisition and publishing energy.

That means a traditional epic fantasy debut should make the editor immediately understand:

### Why this book instead of all the others?

I'd want:

**100–115K words**

**clear Book 1 arc**

**duology/trilogy potential**

# 55. What I Would Build Right Now

If I wanted a traditional epic fantasy with commercial upside, I'd probably combine:

### **Political succession + industrial change + ancient magic returning.**

For example:

> **For three hundred years, the empire's kings have been chosen by a magical sword that kills anyone unworthy to rule. When the old king dies, the sword kills every member of the royal family—and chooses a factory worker who wants nothing to do with the throne just as the empire's conquered provinces begin to revolt.**

Immediately we have:

**reluctant hero**

**succession crisis**

**magic**

**empire**

**industrial setting**

**rebellion**

**political intrigue**

and a concrete question:

> Why did the sword choose him?

That's much easier to sell than:

> A sprawling fantasy saga about an empire threatened by ancient forces.

# 56. Another Concept Direction I'd Favor

### **Epic fantasy + mystery + religion**

> **When the god who has visibly ruled an empire for a thousand years dies, the emperor orders the realm's most disgraced priest to determine who killed him before the other gods discover that divinity can be murdered.**

That provides:

**murder mystery**

**gods**

**religion**

**politics**

**empire**

**world-level stakes**

and a clear narrative engine.

That's the kind of hybridization I think helps epic fantasy stand out now.

# 58. Biggest Strategic Risk

The biggest risk isn't:

> Nobody reads epic fantasy anymore.

That's false.

The risk is:

> **Writing an extremely expensive 160,000-word book that sounds exactly like twenty other fantasy series.**

If you're asking readers to invest:

500 pages,

then another 500,

then another 500,

you need to give them a very strong reason.

The world alone isn't enough.

The characters alone aren't enough.

The magic alone isn't enough.

You need a **story engine**.

# Final Market Recommendation

I still consider Epic Fantasy an attractive genre, but I would approach it with more discipline than almost any category we've discussed.

The broad market is healthy enough to support major fantasy publishing, audiobook consumption is strong, indie fantasy has a substantial commercial ecosystem, and readers clearly remain interested in recognizable epic-fantasy pleasures.

But traditional epic fantasy is currently operating in the shadow of romantasy. In 2025, U.S. fantasy print sales fell after an enormous 2024 surge, while current 2026 U.K. SFF growth is disproportionately driven by romantasy.

So I would not develop:

> **"My version of Lord of the Rings/Game of Thrones/Stormlight."**

I would develop:

> ### **A recognizable epic-fantasy experience built around an unusually specific situation.**

My strongest current directions would be:

**1. Political succession fantasy**

**2. Epic fantasy + mystery**

**3. Industrial/early-modern epic fantasy**

**4. Religious/god-centered epic fantasy**

**5. Exploration fantasy**

**6. Epic fantasy + horror**

**7. Military fantasy with a strong human story**

**8. Cultures and political systems beyond default medieval-European fantasy**

**9. Older or unusual protagonists**

**10. Heroic fantasy that embraces sincerity without becoming simplistic**

The formula I would use is:

# **Simple pitch + complex world.**

Not:

# **Complex pitch + complex world.**

A reader should be able to understand the story in one sentence.

Then you can spend three books proving that the world is much larger than they imagined.

That is still the great promise of Epic Fantasy:

> **Start with one person and one problem. By the end, make the reader feel like the history of an entire world has changed.**
