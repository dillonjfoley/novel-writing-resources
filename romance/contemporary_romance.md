# Contemporary Romance Market Analysis Report

**U.S. market focus — August 2026**

## Executive Summary

Contemporary romance remains one of the strongest commercial fiction categories in publishing. Romance print sales rose **3.9% in 2025 to nearly 44 million units**, after posting even stronger gains earlier in the year, and romance continues to benefit from unusually engaged, socially connected readers. In 2025, Circana reported that romance volume had more than doubled over four years. ([PublishersWeekly.com][1])

For contemporary romance specifically, the opportunity is good—but competition is intense. The genre has moved beyond the period when a cute illustrated cover, quirky job, and enemies-to-lovers premise were enough to stand out.

The strongest commercial formula in 2026 is closer to:

> **Recognizable trope + distinctive situation/setting + intense chemistry + genuine emotional problem + satisfying romantic payoff.**

Readers still want comfort and escapism, but increasingly they also want **big feelings**. Publishers and agents are noticing movement toward more emotional, angsty, complicated stories rather than uniformly light romantic comedies. ([PublishersWeekly.com][2])

# 1. What Counts as Contemporary Romance?

Contemporary romance is essentially a romance occurring in a recognizable present-day world without speculative elements dominating the story.

More importantly, **the romance must be the central narrative**.

A book containing a love story is not necessarily a romance novel.

The reader is buying the story primarily to experience:

**Person A meets Person B → attraction develops → obstacles threaten the relationship → emotional vulnerability increases → the relationship is tested → romantic fulfillment.**

And the genre carries one enormous reader expectation:

### There should be an HEA or HFN.

That means:

**HEA — Happily Ever After**

or

**HFN — Happy For Now**

You can put the characters through misery. You can deal with grief, divorce, abuse recovery, family trauma, illness, career collapse, financial problems or nearly anything else.

But if the central couple permanently breaks up—or one of them dies—it becomes much harder to market the book as genre romance.

That is one of the strongest promises romance makes to its readers.

# 2. Target Audience

There isn't one contemporary-romance demographic anymore.

Sources measuring romance audiences use different methodologies, but they consistently find a significant female skew and a center of gravity somewhere in the 30s. The Romance Writers of America's currently posted reader profile reports an audience that is 82% female with an average reader age of roughly 35–39; newer digital audience modeling for contemporary romance puts the female share closer to 73% and the average around 38. The precise figures shouldn't be treated as interchangeable, but the broader conclusion is reliable. ([RWA][3])

### Primary commercial audience

I would market mainstream contemporary romance primarily toward:

**Women approximately 25–45**

with substantial secondary audiences among:

**Women 18–24**
**Women 45–60+**
**LGBTQ+ romance readers**
**Male romance readers**

The younger side of the audience matters disproportionately for **viral discovery**. NielsenIQ found that romance leads book discovery through video-sharing platforms, and roughly three-quarters of books discovered through video sites were purchased by consumers ages 13–34 in its UK consumer research. ([NIQ][4])

That produces an important distinction:

| Audience | Often responds strongly to                                                          |
| -------- | ----------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| 18–24    | New adult, college, sports, high spice, intense tropes, BookTok hooks               |
| 25–34    | Contemporary, rom-com, sports, workplace, marriage/fake dating, emotional romance   |
| 35–44    | Contemporary, small town, second chance, family-centered stories, romantic suspense |
| 45+      | Small town, family, second chance, mature protagonists, community-centered romance  |

These aren't hard boundaries. They are positioning tendencies.

# 3. What Contemporary Romance Readers Actually Want

The most important thing to understand about romance readers is that **predictability is not inherently a problem**.

Readers frequently *want to know what experience they're purchasing*.

They may actively look for:

> "Fake dating + hockey romance."

or

> "Grumpy sunshine + small town."

or

> "Single dad + nanny + forced proximity."

The question isn't:

**"Has anyone written this trope before?"**

It's:

**"Why should I read your version?"**

That distinction is fundamental to writing romance commercially.

---

# 4. The Reader's Core Needs

## Chemistry

Chemistry is probably the single most important ingredient.

Readers should feel:

**These two people need to be together.**

That can come through sexual tension, humor, emotional intimacy, rivalry, admiration, intellectual compatibility or shared vulnerability.

The relationship cannot feel like the author paired two attractive characters simply because the plot says so.

---

## Emotional payoff

Contemporary romance is increasingly competing on **emotion**, not merely cuteness.

Alyssa Morris of Trellis Literary Management identified "big emotions" and a large emotional journey as a major 2026 romance direction. Current publisher lists likewise mix fun premises with grief, complicated relationships, danger and substantial emotional conflict. ([PublishersWeekly.com][2])

A useful model is:

**Fun premise + painful emotional truth.**

For example:

**Premise:** Two rival wedding planners must fake-date to win an account.

**Emotional truth:** She believes relationships inevitably require giving up part of yourself; he believes anyone who truly knows him eventually leaves.

Now the trope generates an emotional story instead of merely producing funny situations.

Athletes, ranchers, soldiers, executives, doctors, firefighters and other highly competent characters remain useful romantic archetypes because readers immediately understand their appeal.

# 7. Sports Romance

Sports romance deserves special consideration.

It was one of the **fastest-growing romance subjects in 2025**, posting triple-digit growth according to Circana. ([Circana][7])

Hockey in particular became enormous.

But there are signs that the initial gold rush is maturing. Agent Alyssa Morris told *Publishers Weekly* that sports romance appears to be cooling somewhat in 2026, although dedicated audiences remain. ([PublishersWeekly.com][2])

So my assessment is:

### Sports romance is not dying.

It is becoming **crowded**.

"Hockey player falls in love" isn't enough.

You need a sharper differentiator:

**Women's professional sports**
**Olympic athletics**
**Formula racing / motorsports**
**Baseball or basketball**
**Retired athlete**
**Sports medicine**
**Owner/coach/media side of sports**
**Athlete confronting life after their career**
**Queer professional sports romance**

The extraordinary success of Rachel Reid's *Heated Rivalry* illustrates both the continuing power of sports romance and the potential of LGBTQ+ romance. Following its screen adaptation, sales of the novel jumped nearly 1,000% over roughly six weeks; *Publishers Weekly* also reported LGBTQ+ romance print sales rising from about 250,000 units in 2021 to 900,000 in 2025. ([PublishersWeekly.com][8])

# 8. Small-Town Romance

This remains one of the safest contemporary-romance environments.

Current BookBub data is striking here: **Montana hometown, family ranch, small-town Montana, neighbor and New England** are all performing strongly within contemporary romance. ([BookBub Partners Blog][5])

Why does it work?

Because small towns give writers an automatic supporting cast:

The café owner knows everyone.

The protagonist's mother meddles.

The sheriff went to school with the hero.

The heroine's best friend owns the bookstore.

The ex still lives nearby.

The mayor knows the family.

That makes the romance feel embedded in a **world**, which is ideal for series.

Book 1: The rancher
Book 2: His sister
Book 3: The local veterinarian
Book 4: The sheriff
Book 5: The rival ranch owner

Each book receives an HEA while the setting remains familiar.

For commercial indie romance in particular, that structure can be extremely powerful.

# 9. Rom-Coms: Strong but No Longer Automatic

Romantic comedy remains highly viable.

But this is one place where I would be careful.

Circana observed a market shift during 2025 away from some rosier romance categories, including romantic comedy, toward darker or more intense material. ([Circana][7])

That doesn't mean readers stopped liking funny romance.

It means:

### "Cute" is no longer enough differentiation.

The strongest contemporary rom-coms usually combine humor with emotional substance.

Think:

**Funny dialogue + grief**

**absurd premise + fear of commitment**

**workplace comedy + professional insecurity**

**dating disaster + family expectations**

**vacation romance + major life transition**

Emily Henry and Abby Jimenez are useful commercial benchmarks because their books are not simply collections of humorous romantic situations. The emotional lives surrounding the romance matter.

# 10. Heat Level

Contemporary romance supports essentially the entire spectrum.

| Level | Content                         |
| ----- | ------------------------------- |
| 0     | No sexual content               |
| 1     | Kissing / attraction            |
| 2     | Closed-door / fade-to-black     |
| 3     | Open-door but moderate          |
| 4     | Explicit sex                    |
| 5     | Very explicit / erotic elements |

There is a substantial audience for explicit romance, and social media has made readers considerably more comfortable openly seeking "spicy" books. ([PublishersWeekly.com][9])

But:

### You do not need explicit sex to sell romance.

Harlequin currently publishes contemporary lines ranging from wholesome romances to much steamier books, which demonstrates that multiple heat-level markets remain viable. ([Harlequin][6])

What matters more is **matching the promise of the packaging**.

A cute illustrated cover followed by unexpectedly extreme sexual content can create reader dissatisfaction.

Likewise, a highly sensual cover followed by a kiss-only romance may frustrate readers.

# 11. Character Expectations

Modern romance protagonists generally benefit from having identities beyond the relationship.

The protagonist should want something besides:

> "I want a boyfriend."

They might want:

a promotion,
a championship,
financial independence,
custody security,
their business to survive,
a fresh start,
a career change,
reconciliation with family,
creative success,
or simply control over their own life.

Then romance complicates the goal.

That gives you:

**External plot**

plus

**Internal romantic conflict.**

---

# 12. The Modern Romantic Hero

The hyper-controlling alpha hero remains viable in some romance lanes—particularly dark romance—but mainstream contemporary has shifted toward a somewhat different fantasy.

Very broadly, today's successful love interest is often:

**competent + emotionally wounded + fundamentally safe.**

They may appear intimidating.

They may be rich.

They may be famous.

They may be grumpy.

They may be physically imposing.

But underneath that exterior, readers usually need a reason to believe:

> **This person will treat the protagonist differently from everyone else.**

That's the fantasy behind a huge amount of romance.

---

# 13. Emotional Conflict Matters More Than Miscommunication

One of the easiest ways to frustrate modern romance readers is to build the entire third act around something that could be fixed with:

> "Can we talk for thirty seconds?"

Miscommunication can work.

But it needs credible psychological motivation.

Better conflict:

**She gets the career opportunity she has spent ten years pursuing—but it takes her across the country.**

**He wants children; she genuinely doesn't know whether she does.**

**They have fundamentally different relationships with money.**

**His professional future makes their relationship impossible to reveal publicly.**

**She wants to return home; he spent his entire life trying to escape it.**

Now love does not magically remove the problem.

The characters must **change or choose**.

# 14. Diversity and Representation

Contemporary romance has become significantly broader.

Harlequin's current lines explicitly seek characters from varied backgrounds, LGBTQ+ relationships and authors from traditionally marginalized groups. ([Harlequin][6])

There is commercial opportunity in:

interracial romance,
Black romance,
Latino romance,
Asian American romance,
LGBTQ+ romance,
disabled protagonists,
neurodivergent protagonists,
older protagonists,
plus-size protagonists,
divorced protagonists,
single parents,
and characters from varied economic backgrounds.

The key is avoiding the impression that diversity was added as a marketing checkbox.

Specificity beats generic representation.

# 15. Ideal Length

There is no universal contemporary-romance length, but for a mainstream single-title romance I would generally target:

### **75,000–95,000 words**

with roughly **80,000–90,000** as a very comfortable commercial range.

Current Harlequin examples illustrate the variation: Special Edition targets exactly 70,000 words, while the newer Afterglow program asks for at least 75,000. Category lines can run considerably shorter. ([Harlequin][6])

My practical recommendation:

| Type                             | Approximate target |
| -------------------------------- | -----------------: |
| Short/category romance           |             50–70K |
| Standard contemporary            |             75–90K |
| Big emotional contemporary       |            85–100K |
| Romance with major subplot/world |            90–105K |

Once a contemporary romance goes significantly past 110K, I would want a strong reason.

# 16. POV

Three approaches dominate:

### Dual first person

**Her chapter → His chapter**

Extremely useful for commercial romance because readers experience both characters falling in love.

### Single first person

Good when mystery about the love interest is part of the appeal.

### Dual close third

Slightly more traditional-feeling but still highly effective.

For a commercially driven contemporary romance, I would generally favor:

### **Dual first-person POV**

unless the story gains tension by withholding one character's feelings.

---

# 17. When Should the Couple Meet?

Early.

Preferably very early.

Readers bought a romance because they want to watch the relationship unfold.

You generally don't want to spend 70 pages introducing the protagonist's workplace before the love interest appears.

Even Harlequin's current Love Inspired guidance explicitly asks that its romantic leads meet by approximately the end of chapter one. ([Harlequin][10])

For mainstream contemporary romance, I would aim for:

**First encounter: Chapters 1–3.**

# 18. The Cover Market

Illustration remains dominant in much of contemporary romance, particularly rom-coms.

But the generic "flat cartoon couple against a pastel background" style is becoming increasingly saturated.

*Publishers Weekly* reported in 2025 that publishers were experimenting with more distinctive illustration, character specificity, collage, retro aesthetics and especially bold typography while maintaining recognizable romance signals. ([PublishersWeekly.com][11])

### Current strong directions

**Bold typography**

**Limited character illustration**

**Retro illustration**

**Editorial-style illustration**

**Distinctive environmental imagery**

**Setting-centered covers**

**Object/symbol-driven covers**

And importantly:

### A cover should communicate the exact flavor of romance.

A cozy Maine romance shouldn't resemble a dark billionaire romance.

A spicy hockey romance shouldn't look indistinguishable from women's fiction.

Genre familiarity gets the click.

Distinctiveness gets the attention.

---

# 19. Packaging Is Becoming More Important

Romance readers increasingly treat books as collectible objects.

Special editions, sprayed edges, alternate covers, illustrated endpapers and bonus material have grown substantially, with publishers explicitly connecting the trend to romance fandom and social-media shelf culture. ([PublishersWeekly.com][12])

The rise of dedicated romance bookstores makes this even more important.

The American Booksellers Association told *Publishers Weekly* that **232 brick-and-mortar or mobile romance bookstores had opened in the U.S. over the preceding three years**, including 54 in the first part of 2026 alone. ([PublishersWeekly.com][13])

That is a remarkable indicator of how much romance has moved from a primarily private reading category into a visible **fandom culture**.

# 21. Series Strategy

Contemporary romance is exceptionally well suited to **interconnected standalones**.

This may be the strongest series structure in the genre.

### Book 1

Character A + Character B → HEA.

### Book 2

Supporting Character C + new love interest → HEA.

### Book 3

Supporting Character D + Character E → HEA.

Readers receive closure every time while remaining invested in the world.

Excellent series frameworks include:

**five siblings**

**small-town businesses**

**hockey teammates**

**ranching family**

**firehouse**

**wedding party**

**professional sports team**

**friend group**

**apartment building**

**restaurant staff**

**hospital staff**

**family-owned company**

The world becomes an acquisition engine for later books.

# 22. Saturated Concepts

I would be cautious about pitching these **without a distinctive hook**:

### Generic hockey romance

Still viable, but extremely competitive.

### Generic billionaire romance

Evergreen but crowded.

### Generic enemies-to-lovers office romance

Needs something more.

### Generic small-town cowboy

Strong market, enormous competition.

### Generic illustrated rom-com

The packaging itself no longer provides differentiation.

### "She's quirky; he's grumpy."

Not enough anymore.

None of these concepts are dead.

They simply require a stronger **second hook**.

# 24. The "Trope Stack" Strategy

This is probably the most useful technique if you're deliberately designing a commercial romance.

Don't start with:

> I want to write a romance.

Start with four layers.

**Setting**

Small Montana tourist town.

*

**Primary trope**

Second chance.

*

**Secondary trope**

Single dad.

*

**Situation**

They're forced to run the town's winter festival together.

Now you have:

> **A single-dad second-chance romance about former high-school sweethearts forced to save their Montana hometown's failing winter festival.**

That is a product.

The generic version—

> A woman returns home and reconnects with an old boyfriend.

—is merely a premise.

# 25. What Readers Will Punish

Romance readers are unusually genre-literate.

Common sources of poor reader response include:

**No believable chemistry**

**The romance being secondary to another plot**

**Love interest appears too late**

**Third-act breakup created by stupidity**

**Hero behaves abusively in a book marketed as wholesome contemporary romance**

**Characters forgive enormous betrayals too easily**

**False advertising about spice**

**A purported enemies-to-lovers relationship where they are mildly irritated for two chapters**

**A slow burn without enough tension**

**A 100-page setup before anything romantic happens**

**An ending without sufficient romantic payoff**

And probably most importantly:

### Failing to deliver the trope you advertised.

If you sell readers **enemies to lovers**, they want enemies.

If you sell **forced proximity**, trap them together.

If you sell **only one bed**, somebody better be sleeping in that bed.

# 28. The Ideal 2027 Contemporary Romance Pitch

If I were developing a new contemporary romance specifically for commercial potential, I would prioritize:

**Characters:** late 20s to late 30s
**Length:** 80–90K
**POV:** dual first person
**Structure:** standalone within an interconnected world
**Heat:** moderate-to-high unless deliberately targeting sweet romance
**Setting:** visually distinctive location or community
**Primary trope:** immediately recognizable
**Secondary trope:** recognizable
**Emotional hook:** serious enough to make readers genuinely care
**Tone:** funny/warm without avoiding sadness
**Ending:** emphatic HEA
**Cover:** illustrated or typography-forward, but distinctive rather than generic
**Series opportunity:** obvious supporting characters capable of carrying later books

And I would want to be able to describe the entire project in one sentence containing **at least three hooks**.

For example:

> **A recently divorced wedding photographer returns to her family's failing Montana ranch and discovers the only man willing to help save it is the high-school sweetheart she left at the altar fifteen years earlier.**

Immediately we have:

**Montana**
**family ranch**
**second chance**
**returning home**
**older/more mature protagonists**
**wedding profession irony**
**emotional history**

That is exactly the sort of concept architecture I would favor in today's market.

# Bottom Line

**Contemporary romance remains an excellent genre to publish in.**

But I would not chase "romance" generally.

I'd chase a **specific reader fantasy**.

The market currently rewards books that make readers immediately think:

> **"Oh, I know what kind of book this is—and I want that."**

Then the novel needs to surprise them through character rather than by refusing to give them what they came for.

The safest commercial philosophy is:

### Familiar trope. Fresh execution. Big emotion. Strong chemistry. Guaranteed payoff.

That is contemporary romance's sweet spot in 2026.

If I were building a contemporary-romance publishing strategy today, I'd be particularly interested in **second-chance romance, single-parent romance, relationship/marriage stories, distinctive small-town settings, queer contemporary romance, and alternatives to overcrowded hockey/billionaire concepts**. Those give you recognizable romance hooks without forcing you directly into the most saturated parts of the market. ([BookBub Partners Blog][5])
