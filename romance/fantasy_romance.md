# Fantasy Romance / Romantasy Market Analysis

**U.S.-focused, with current international publishing signals — August 2026**

## Executive Summary

Fantasy romance remains one of the biggest commercial opportunities in fiction, but it is entering a **more mature and competitive phase**.

In 2025, Circana identified romantasy as one of the fastest-growing romance subjects in the U.S., with triple-digit growth during the first part of the year. Rebecca Yarros's *Onyx Storm* alone sold nearly 1.7 million U.S. print copies during 2025. At the same time, overall fantasy print sales fell 8.7%, showing how concentrated success can be around highly commercial titles. ([Circana][1])

The latest 2026 international signals remain extremely strong. In the U.K., SFF sales were running **16% ahead of 2025** as of August, with **39 of the top 50 SFF books classified as romantasy**; more than half of the SFF hardcovers scheduled for autumn 2026 were romantasy titles. ([The Bookseller][2])

But the market is crowded. Agents at the 2026 Bologna Book Fair described romantasy as somewhat saturated while simultaneously reporting continuing demand, particularly for **high-concept books that bring something new to the category**. ([PublishersWeekly.com][3])

### Overall market assessment

| Factor                          | Assessment |
| ------------------------------- | ---------- |
| Reader demand                   | ★★★★★      |
| Traditional publishing interest | ★★★★★      |
| Indie potential                 | ★★★★★      |
| BookTok potential               | ★★★★★      |
| Series potential                | ★★★★★      |
| Audiobook potential             | ★★★★★      |
| Special-edition potential       | ★★★★★      |
| Competition                     | ★★★★★      |
| Ease of breaking in             | ★★☆☆☆      |
| Need for distinctive concept    | ★★★★★      |

**Bottom line:** I would absolutely consider fantasy romance commercially viable for 2027–2029—but I would not enter the market with a generic ACOTAR/Fourth Wing imitation.

# 1. First: Fantasy Romance vs. Romantic Fantasy vs. Romantasy

This distinction matters.

**Romantasy** has become an umbrella marketing term covering books that combine significant fantasy and romance elements. Industry definitions remain somewhat elastic. ([PublishersWeekly.com][4])

But from a publishing perspective, I would divide it this way:

| Category             | Primary engine                    |
| -------------------- | --------------------------------- |
| **Fantasy Romance**  | The love story                    |
| **Romantic Fantasy** | The fantasy story                 |
| **Romantasy**        | Commercial umbrella covering both |

A recent *Publishers Weekly* review of Jenna Moreci's *How to Write Romantasy* makes essentially the same distinction: fantasy romance puts greater emphasis on the lovers, while romantic fantasy gives more weight to worldbuilding. ([PublishersWeekly.com][5])

The easiest test is:

### Fantasy Romance

If you remove the romance, **the novel falls apart**.

> A witch must marry the enemy prince to stop a war, but falling in love with him could destroy both kingdoms.

### Romantic Fantasy

If you remove the romance, **you still have a fantasy story**.

> A witch must recover five magical artifacts before an ancient god awakens; along the way she falls in love with a rival mage.

That distinction affects reader expectations enormously.

---

# 2. Target Audience

The core fantasy-romance market heavily skews female, but spans a surprisingly large age range.

Publishers have described romantasy's audience as including teen girls, younger adult women, millennials, and increasingly older adult romance/fantasy readers. The rise of crossover and New Adult publishing has made the boundary between YA and adult fantasy romance especially blurry. ([PublishersWeekly.com][6])

For commercial adult romantasy, I would think primarily in terms of:

### Core audience

**Women approximately 18–39**

with particularly strong engagement among:

**18–24:** New Adult / BookTok / high-emotion / high-spice
**25–34:** adult romantasy / epic romance / darker themes
**35–44:** established fantasy and romance crossover readers

There are obviously readers outside those groups.

But if you are designing **commercial packaging, tropes and positioning**, women roughly 18–35 remain the center of gravity.

---

# 3. What These Readers Want

Fantasy-romance readers essentially want two fantasies simultaneously.

They want:

### The romantic fantasy

> Someone extraordinarily powerful chooses me above everyone else.

And:

### The adventure fantasy

> I discover that I am capable of far more than anyone imagined.

That combination explains why the genre works so well.

A successful protagonist frequently experiences both:

**romantic awakening**

and

**power awakening.**

The emotional fantasy might therefore look like:

> She discovers she has forbidden magic—and the terrifying warrior assigned to kill people like her becomes the one person willing to burn the kingdom down to protect her.

That is almost a distilled romantasy fantasy.

---

# 4. The Central Reader Promise

Fantasy romance needs to deliver both:

## **Swoon**

and

## **Wonder**

A romance with superficial fantasy decoration usually disappoints fantasy readers.

A detailed fantasy story with three romantic scenes may disappoint romance readers.

This is probably the hardest balancing act in the genre.

The best books make the fantasy plot **create romantic tension**.

For example:

Bad integration:

> They're fighting a dragon. Later, they have sex.

Better integration:

> Their magical bond means she feels every wound he receives while fighting the dragon.

Now fantasy and romance are part of the same mechanism.

# 5. The Biggest Romance Tropes

These remain highly recognizable:

### Tier 1 — Extremely marketable

**Forbidden love**

**Forced proximity**

**Fated mates**

**Slow burn**

**Marriage of convenience / arranged marriage**

**Rivals to lovers**

**Reluctant allies**

### Tier 2 — Strong supporting tropes

**Bodyguard/protector**

**Touch her and die**

**Who did this to you?**

**One bed**

**One horse**

**Hidden identity**

**Secret royalty**

**Childhood enemies**

**Second chance**

**Captor/captive — primarily darker romantasy**

**Soul bond / magical bond**

**Love triangle**

**Witch × witch hunter**

**Monster × monster hunter**

**Princess × enemy commander**

**Knight × royal**

**Dragon rider × rival rider**

**Mage × anti-magic warrior**

**Human × immortal**

**God × mortal**

**Fae × human**

**Necromancer × paladin**

**Pirate × royal**

The current market still explicitly uses enemies-to-lovers and slow-burn positioning in major acquisitions. A July 2026 deal, for example, paired a siren aboard a cursed pirate ship with its enigmatic captain in a slow-burn enemies-to-lovers romance. ([PublishersWeekly.com][7])

So dragons certainly aren't dead.

---

# 7. The Best Strategy: Combine Romance Tropes With Fantasy Hooks

This is where I think authors should concentrate.

Don't pitch:

> An enemies-to-lovers romantasy.

There are thousands.

Pitch:

> **A disgraced dragon healer is forced to magically bond with the enemy prince whose dragon killed her brother—and discovers that if either man or beast dies, the other three die with them.**

Now you've stacked:

**Dragons**

* **enemies to lovers**
* **magical bond**
* **forced proximity**
* **enemy prince**
* **family revenge**
* **life-or-death stakes**

That's a book readers can identify quickly.

---

# 8. Fae: Still Viable, But Very Crowded

Fae romance is not dead.

Sarah J. Maas has created such an enormous readership that fae will probably remain part of fantasy romance for years.

But I would consider:

> **Human woman enters dangerous fae court and falls for incredibly powerful fae prince**

one of the most saturated concepts in the genre.

You now need a strong second hook.

Instead of merely:

**fae prince**

try:

**fae criminal court**

**fae plague**

**fae Wild West**

**fae murder mystery**

**fae naval empire**

**fae religious war**

**fae industrial revolution**

**fae political marriage**

The species isn't the hook anymore.

The **situation** needs to be.

---

# 9. Dragons: Still Commercial

Dragons are similarly crowded—but currently stronger as a marketing shorthand than I would have expected several years after *Fourth Wing*.

Rebecca Yarros remained one of publishing's biggest sales drivers in 2025, and BookBub's newest fantasy data has "dragon rider" outperforming its category average. ([PublishersWeekly.com][9])

Again:

### Don't avoid dragons.

Avoid:

> **Young woman goes to dragon academy and falls for dangerous dragon rider.**

That immediately invites *Fourth Wing* comparisons.

Instead ask:

**What haven't readers done with dragons lately?**

Dragon veterinarians.

Dragon smugglers.

Dragon archaeologists.

Dragon diplomats.

Dragon priests.

Dragons used in naval warfare.

Dragon racing.

Dragon conservation.

A world where dragons are going extinct.

A country where killing dragons is sacred.

A romance between people on opposing sides of dragon conservation.

That's differentiation.

---

# 10. Knights May Be Especially Interesting

This is one area I would watch closely.

During 2025, publishers noticed a surge in **female-knight fantasy**, with at least ten knight-centered titles appearing during the year. Editors connected the trend directly to romantasy's appetite for highly romantic heroic archetypes. ([PublishersWeekly.com][10])

I think there's considerable runway around:

**knights**

**paladins**

**holy orders**

**female knights**

**bodyguards**

**royal guards**

**knight × witch**

**knight × enemy princess**

**knight × dragon**

**knight × necromancer**

It delivers the competence fantasy readers enjoy without going back to another generic fae prince.

---

# 11. Mythology, Gothic Fantasy and Retellings

These are also promising expansion areas.

Current publishing lists include gothic Shakespearean romantasy, gender-swapped *Phantom of the Opera* retellings, demigod stories, *Wizard of Oz*-inspired romantic fantasy, vampires, witches and other hybrid concepts. ([PublishersWeekly.com][11])

This reflects a larger shift:

### Romantasy is expanding outward.

The Bookseller reported in March 2026 that publishers still had romantasy firmly on their radar but were looking for the category to **broaden its scope**. ([The Bookseller][12])

That's a healthy sign.

I would rather develop:

> **Jane Austen × necromancers**

than:

> **ACOTAR with different character names.**

---

# 12. High Stakes Are Important

Unlike contemporary romance, fantasy romance thrives on exaggerated stakes.

The relationship can determine:

**who rules the kingdom**

**whether a war ends**

**whether magic survives**

**whether one character lives**

**whether an ancient god awakens**

**whether two species go to war**

That's part of the genre fantasy.

Romantasy has historically leaned heavily into life-or-death situations: rival kingdoms, dangerous tournaments, forbidden pairings and characters whose romantic decisions may literally affect the fate of their worlds. ([PublishersWeekly.com][4])

The trick is making the **emotional stakes equal the physical ones.**

---

# 13. The Love Interest

The modern romantasy love interest is often some variation of:

### **Dangerous to the world. Safe for her.**

Common characteristics:

**powerful**

**competent**

**feared**

**emotionally guarded**

**morally gray**

**protective**

**high social status or unusual power**

**traumatic history**

**capacity for violence**

But critically:

> The reader needs evidence for why the protagonist should love this person.

"He's hot and powerful" isn't sufficient characterization.

A compelling love interest needs:

**values**

**vulnerability**

**desires**

**moral limits**

**something to lose**

and preferably an identity independent of the protagonist.

---

# 14. The Heroine

A highly familiar romantasy protagonist is:

> Young woman discovers she possesses extraordinary powers.

This works.

But it has become **extremely common**.

Particularly familiar:

**orphan heroine**

**secret royal blood**

**hidden magical ability**

**initially weak but secretly strongest person alive**

**special magical bond**

**everyone underestimated her**

You can still use these.

But I'd recommend complicating them.

Instead of:

> She discovers she's the most powerful mage alive.

Consider:

> She discovers she has the only kind of magic nobody wants.

Or:

> Her power is extraordinary—but useless in combat.

Or:

> Everyone already knows she's powerful, and she desperately wishes she weren't.

Readers don't require completely original tropes.

They require **an interesting variation.**

---

# 15. Magic Systems

Fantasy-romance audiences generally tolerate softer magic systems than hardcore epic-fantasy readers.

But the magic should still have internal logic.

Ideally establish:

**What can magic do?**

**Who gets it?**

**What does it cost?**

**What can't it do?**

**What happens when someone abuses it?**

And, most importantly for this genre:

### Can magic complicate romance?

Excellent mechanisms include:

**bonded magic**

**shared dreams**

**life bonds**

**truth magic**

**emotion sensing**

**magical oaths**

**curse-based marriage**

**powers that interact**

**enemies who must combine their magic**

The best magic system can actually **generate the romantic plot.**

---

# 16. Heat Level

Adult romantasy has a strong market for open-door sex.

But "romantasy = smut" is incorrect.

There's room for:

**0 — no sex**

**1 — kisses**

**2 — closed-door**

**3 — moderate open-door**

**4 — explicit**

**5 — very explicit**

The important thing is positioning.

The 2026 marketplace continues to feature prominently advertised **"steamy slow-burn romantasy"**, while publishers are also releasing lower-heat fantasy romances. ([PublishersWeekly.com][13])

For adult commercial romantasy, I would currently call:

### **3–4 spice**

the broadest commercial sweet spot.

But I'd rather publish a fantastic closed-door romance than insert four graphic sex scenes purely because TikTok seems to expect them.

---

# 17. YA vs. New Adult vs. Adult

This is particularly messy right now.

The publishing industry itself acknowledges that YA/New Adult/adult crossover boundaries have become increasingly difficult to define. The 2026 Bologna reporting specifically notes romantasy and fanfiction helping turn New Adult/crossover into a major commercial space. ([PublishersWeekly.com][3])

Very roughly:

| Category             | Typical protagonist | Content                                       |
| -------------------- | ------------------: | --------------------------------------------- |
| YA                   |               16–18 | coming-of-age; generally less explicit        |
| New Adult crossover  |               18–24 | independence, sexuality, first adult identity |
| Adult                |                20s+ | broad                                         |
| Adult epic romantasy |            20s–40s+ | broad                                         |

I would be cautious about writing a 19-year-old protagonist, explicit sex, adult political themes and 120,000 words and assuming it's YA.

That's increasingly an **adult/New Adult crossover** book.

# 18. Word Count

Fantasy romance naturally runs longer than contemporary romance.

My targets would be:

| Type                   | Recommended range |
| ---------------------- | ----------------: |
| YA romantasy           |           80–100K |
| Adult fantasy romance  |       **90–115K** |
| Adult romantic fantasy |       **95–120K** |
| Epic romantasy         |          105–130K |
| Indie fantasy romance  |           80–120K |

For a debut intended for traditional publishing, I'd be happiest around:

### **95,000–110,000 words.**

A 145,000-word manuscript is not automatically unpublishable.

But you're increasing production costs and asking an agent/editor to take a bigger risk.

---

# 19. POV

The dominant commercial choices are:

### First-person single POV

Extremely immersive.

Good for:

**mystery around love interest**

**power discovery**

**unreliable perceptions**

**slow burn**

### Dual first person

Excellent when the romance is the real heart of the novel.

Allows readers to watch **both people fall.**

### Close third person

Works especially well if your fantasy plot is larger and you're leaning toward romantic fantasy.

My rule:

> **The more romance-focused the story is, the more attractive dual POV becomes.**

> **The more mystery-focused the love interest is, the stronger single POV becomes.**

---

# 20. HEA Expectations

This depends heavily on positioning.

If you're selling the book explicitly as **fantasy romance**, romance readers expect eventual romantic fulfillment.

That doesn't necessarily mean every installment ends happily.

Book 1 can end with:

betrayal,

separation,

capture,

war,

or even apparent death.

But across the romantic arc, readers generally expect the couple to get their payoff.

If you're writing **romantic fantasy**, you have more freedom to kill the love interest, destroy the relationship or end tragically.

That's another reason labeling matters.

---

# 21. Series Strategy

Fantasy romance is exceptionally series-friendly.

The two most useful structures are:

### One-couple epic

Book 1 — attraction
Book 2 — relationship deepens
Book 3 — relationship/world climax

This is the **ACOTAR/Fourth Wing-style model.**

Or:

### Interconnected couples

Book 1 — Couple A
Book 2 — Couple B
Book 3 — Couple C

Same fantasy universe.

This gives you the romance-reader advantage of a new couple while retaining fantasy-world investment.

For indie publishing, I particularly like this model because every supporting character can become the hook for another release.

---

# 22. Cover Expectations

Romantasy packaging is unusually important.

Current covers commonly signal the category through:

**ornate typography**

**botanical borders**

**weapons**

**crowns**

**dragons**

**snakes**

**moons**

**magical symbols**

**gothic architecture**

**metallic foil**

**black/gold palettes**

**illustrated creatures**

Publishers explicitly treat mystical objects surrounded by ornate/floral imagery as immediate romantasy signaling. ([PublishersWeekly.com][14])

But the biggest packaging phenomenon is the **special edition**.

Sprayed edges, stenciled edges, foil, maps, endpapers, reversible jackets and bonus scenes have become particularly important in romance and romantasy. Publishers and subscription boxes are increasingly treating deluxe hardcover editions almost like a separate format. ([PublishersWeekly.com][15])

For this genre:

### The book itself is merchandise.

That matters more here than in almost any other adult-fiction category.

# 23. Self-Publishing Potential

**Excellent.**

Romance already has an unusually developed indie ecosystem, and publishers are increasingly watching successful self-published romance and romantasy authors for traditional acquisitions. *Publishers Weekly* described this self-pub-to-traditional pipeline as significant in 2026, while international scouts specifically noted that publishers monitor self-published romantasy spaces closely. ([PublishersWeekly.com][16])

Fantasy romance works particularly well for indie authors because of:

**Kindle Unlimited**

**rapid series reading**

**trope-based purchasing**

**BookTok**

**cover-driven browsing**

**special editions**

**audiobooks**

**direct sales**

**reader communities**

But production costs are greater than contemporary romance because readers may expect:

maps,

longer books,

more elaborate covers,

and stronger worldbuilding.

# 24. Saturated Concepts

I'd be most careful with:

### Fae prince + human heroine

Still commercial. Extremely familiar.

### Dragon-rider academy

Very difficult to avoid *Fourth Wing* comparisons.

### Generic magical trials

Especially:

> compete in deadly tournament → enemies-to-lovers prince.

### Shadow-powered morally gray prince

There are a lot of them.

### Elemental magic with four kingdoms

Still works, but feels familiar quickly.

### Secretly super-powerful orphan

You need differentiation.

### "Beauty and the Beast but fantasy"

One of the genre's foundational archetypes, but needs a strong transformation.

### Generic Greek mythology retelling

The mythology alone isn't enough anymore.

# 25. Areas With Strong Opportunity

If I were acquiring fantasy romance today, I'd be especially interested in:

### Knight romance

Strong visual identity and underused compared with fae.

### Non-Western mythological fantasy romance

Not simply reskinning medieval Europe.

### Gothic romantasy

Haunted castles, curses, ghosts, vampires, ancient families.

### Monster romance with substantial fantasy plot

Not merely erotic monster romance.

### Fantasy mystery + romance

Murder inside a magical court.

### Fantasy adventure + romance

Indiana Jones rather than another royal palace.

### Pirate romantasy

A very natural combination of forced proximity, adventure and dangerous romance.

### Gods and mortals

Particularly inventive original pantheons.

### Adult protagonists 30+

Still comparatively underused.

### Cozy romantasy

Lower stakes but strong relationship/world comfort.

### Queer romantasy

Especially where the fantasy concept itself feels fresh rather than simply swapping genders into familiar stories.

### Fantasy built around unusual professions

Magical physicians.

Monster veterinarians.

Royal architects.

Curse lawyers.

Dragon undertakers.

Magical cartographers.

Potion smugglers.

Necromantic detectives.

Give the protagonist a **job**, not merely a destiny.

# 27. The Ideal Commercial Romantasy Concept

If I were intentionally developing one for the current market, I'd aim for:

**Audience:** Adult/New Adult crossover
**Protagonist:** 21–35
**Length:** 95–110K
**POV:** single or dual first
**Romance:** slow burn
**Heat:** 3–4
**Series:** planned duology or trilogy
**Primary romance trope:** immediately recognizable
**Fantasy hook:** unusual enough to differentiate the project
**Worldbuilding:** accessible rather than encyclopedic
**Magic:** simple rules with major emotional consequences
**Love interest:** dangerous/competent but deeply characterized
**Ending:** satisfying while leaving a larger series question
**Cover:** highly collectible
**Pitch:** understandable in one or two sentences

Most importantly:

### Don't sell "a fantasy world."

Sell a **fantasy situation**.

Compare:

> A young woman discovers magical powers and falls for a mysterious prince while fighting an evil empire.

versus:

> **Every royal marriage requires the bride and groom to hunt and kill a dragon together. When a pacifist princess is forced to marry the enemy king responsible for her brother's death, they discover their ceremonial dragon is actually the last surviving god.**

The second gives me:

**arranged marriage**

**enemies to lovers**

**dragon**

**enemy king**

**forced proximity**

**family vengeance**

**religious mystery**

**political stakes**

and an obvious visual identity.

That's what I would look for.

# 28. What I Think Is Happening to the Market

I **do not** think we're witnessing the end of romantasy.

I think we're moving from:

### Phase 1

**"Romantasy itself is the hook."**

to:

### Phase 2

**"What kind of romantasy is it?"**

That's actually healthy.

Current publishers are still launching romantasy imprints, acquiring new duologies, and building crossover lists around the category. At the same time, scouts and agents are explicitly saying the bar for originality has risen. ([PublishersWeekly.com][17])

That means a writer starting now shouldn't ask:

> **"How do I write the next Fourth Wing?"**

Ask:

> **"What's the fantasy-romance concept readers haven't been seeing five times a month?"**

## Final Market Recommendation

For a **2027–2028 fantasy-romance project**, my highest-interest formula would be:

### **Recognizable romance trope + underused fantasy archetype + strong profession/situation + accessible magic + large emotional stakes.**

For example:

**Enemies to lovers + lady knight + necromancer detective + murder mystery**

**Arranged marriage + pirate queen + sea monster mythology**

**Second chance + royal dragon veterinarian + dying dragons**

**Forbidden romance + monster hunter + vampire murder investigation**

**Marriage of convenience + rival magical cartographers + exploration adventure**

**Bodyguard romance + disgraced paladin + cursed queen**

Those feel more commercially interesting to me right now than another straightforward **fae prince / magical trials / secret powers / shadow daddy** story.

And if I had to pick **one underexploited direction with breakout potential**, I'd look very closely at **fantasy romance crossed with another strong genre engine—mystery, adventure, gothic horror, heist, piracy, or political thriller.** That gives readers all the familiar pleasures of romantasy while giving your book an identity beyond its romance tropes.
