I went through the attached *Amy Tan Teaches Fiction, Memory, and Imagination* instructor guide and supplemented it with Tan's public MasterClass material, interviews, PBS, Rutgers, the National Endowment for the Arts, *Poets & Writers*, and other interviews about her process.

Compared with Aaron Sorkin, Tan approaches writing from almost the opposite direction. Sorkin tends to begin with the **dramatic engine**—intention and obstacle. Tan tends to begin with **consciousness, emotional memory, curiosity, and contradiction**. For her, fiction is a method of discovering something that she did not completely understand before she began writing.

# Amy Tan's Method of Writing

## Think in guideposts, not rules

One of the most revealing things Tan says at the beginning of her MasterClass is that she doesn't want to give writers rules. She wants to give them **guideposts**.

That distinction fits almost everything else she teaches. She experiments with point of view. She discovers endings while drafting. She rewrites beginnings after discovering what the novel became. She lets research produce unexpected connections. She allows memory to mutate into imagination.

Her process therefore isn't:

> Design everything correctly and then execute the plan.

It's closer to:

> Give yourself enough direction to enter the story, then remain curious about what the story reveals.

That makes Tan particularly useful for novelists who lean toward discovery writing. ([MasterClass][1])

---

# Fiction is a way of finding truth

This is probably the central idea behind Tan's philosophy.

She rejects the notion that because fiction contains invented events it is therefore merely "lies." The attached guide describes fiction as one of the ways she searches for truth, particularly truths about human nature. 

But the truth she's after isn't necessarily factual truth.

It's **emotional truth**.

Tan recently described the process on PBS as searching through fiction until she reaches something true about **how an experience felt and why it felt that way**. The more specific and personal that truth becomes, she says, the more surprisingly universal it can become for readers. ([PBS][2])

So the Tan question isn't simply:

> What happened?

It's:

> **What did this mean to me?**

And then:

> **What might fiction allow me to discover about why it mattered?**

That difference is fundamental.

---

# Use your life—but don't become imprisoned by what actually happened

Tan draws heavily from experience, but she does **not** recommend faithfully transcribing autobiography into fiction.

She learned this herself. Her first attempted story concerned her father's death, but she tried to preserve events exactly as they happened. She later concluded that adhering too rigidly to reality made the story stiff and inflexible. ([MasterClass][3])

Instead, preserve the **emotional memory**.

The attached guide puts this explicitly: memory isn't valuable primarily because it lets you remember dates, locations, and factual details. Its value to fiction comes from recovering the emotion attached to the experience. 

Suppose you remember being humiliated in seventh grade.

Tan's approach isn't necessarily:

> Reconstruct exactly who said what in the classroom on October 12.

Instead ask:

> What did humiliation feel like?

Maybe:

your face became hot;

you suddenly couldn't look at anyone;

you pretended not to care;

you hated the person who laughed;

you hated yourself for caring;

you remembered it twenty years later while doing something completely unrelated.

You can give **that emotional truth** to a fictional character in an entirely different circumstance.

That's how autobiography becomes fiction.

---

# Memory is subjective—and that's useful

Tan doesn't treat memory as a perfect recording.

The attached guide actually encourages writers to compare their recollection of an event with the recollections of other people who experienced it. The discrepancies reveal how much perspective shapes memory. 

For fiction writers, that's extremely useful.

Two people experience the same divorce.

One remembers abandonment.

One remembers liberation.

A child remembers silence.

A parent remembers constant arguing.

Nobody necessarily has to be lying.

They are experiencing different internal stories.

That gives Tan's approach a useful principle:

**Don't merely ask what happened. Ask what each character believes happened.**

Those two things can be very different.

---

# Look for contradictions in people

This may be Tan's best concrete character-development exercise.

She argues that truths about human beings frequently exist as contradictions in which **both things are true**.

The attached guide gives examples such as someone who dislikes violence but loves hockey, or someone who seems cheerful but frequently cries. It then asks the writer to identify paradoxes in people they know. 

Apply that directly to characters:

A courageous soldier is terrified of disappointing his father.

A gentle mother can be ruthlessly controlling.

A selfish thief is extraordinarily loyal.

A devout priest privately doubts God.

A woman desperate for independence cannot bear being alone.

A king who hates cruelty orders executions because he believes mercy will cause civil war.

Tan's character philosophy becomes:

**While this is true about the character, this seemingly contradictory thing is also true.**

That is a remarkably effective antidote to flat characterization.

---

# Don't simplify human beings into an argument

Tan's interest in contradiction extends beyond character quirks.

She is interested in people whose beliefs don't fit neatly together.

In an NEA interview, she described character perspective as a kind of personal theory of how the universe works: what someone notices, what they believe causes things to happen, and how those beliefs emerge from what matters emotionally to them. Contradictory beliefs are not necessarily flaws in characterization; they can be evidence of individuality. ([National Endowment for the Arts][4])

So instead of deciding:

> This character represents tradition.

Tan's approach would encourage something more complicated:

> She treasures the tradition that made her feel connected to her mother, despises the tradition that limited her choices, and doesn't recognize that she is imposing another version of that tradition on her daughter.

Now you have a person.

---

# Voice is your consciousness

This is another major pillar of Tan's philosophy.

She doesn't define authorial voice mainly as:

sentence length,

word choice,

quirky dialogue,

or recognizable prose tricks.

Those are manifestations of voice.

The deeper source is **consciousness**.

Tan describes writerly voice as what you notice, what you consider contradictory, what you believe, what experiences shaped those beliefs, and the questions about life that fascinate you. ([MasterClass][5])

In her Rutgers interview, she says voice comes from what you uniquely notice about life—essentially your particular guide to the universe. ([Rutgers University][6])

This provides a much better way to search for a writing voice than trying to manufacture one.

Don't ask:

> How can I sound distinctive?

Ask:

> **What do I notice that another writer might not notice?**

And:

> **What questions about life do I keep returning to?**

Your voice grows out of the answers.

---

# Your narrator's voice is not necessarily your voice

Tan makes an important distinction between **the writer's consciousness** and **the narrator's voice**.

Your interests determine why you're examining a particular situation.

But your narrator doesn't have to believe what you believe.

The narrator has their own consciousness.

Their voice emerges from:

what they notice;

what they overlook;

what they believe;

what they misunderstand;

what they fear;

what they assume;

and how they interpret events.

That creates a useful character exercise.

Put two characters in exactly the same room.

One notices the expensive furniture.

One notices that the windows don't open.

One notices that everyone is lying.

One notices the dog sleeping under the table.

You've already begun differentiating their voices.

([MasterClass][5])

---

# Get the voice onto the page quickly

Tan puts enormous importance on beginnings.

In her Rutgers interview she says that **voice needs to arrive quickly**. ([Rutgers University][6])

And when discussing books she enjoys reading, she says a distinctive intelligence in the voice is one of the things that makes her continue. Her experience judging fiction also taught her how heavily the opening line and first paragraph influence a reader's expectations. ([The Daily Beast][7])

So a Tan-style first page isn't merely trying to produce "action."

It is establishing:

**Who is telling me this?**

**What do they notice?**

**How do they interpret the world?**

**Why should I want to spend hundreds of pages inside this consciousness?**

That's particularly useful advice for literary fiction, first-person fantasy, romance, mystery, and character-heavy novels.

---

# But the opening you draft first may not be the real opening

Here's one of the most interesting things about Tan's process.

She has said that her openings frequently change and may effectively be **the last part she writes**.

Why?

Because after finishing the novel she finally understands everything that happened, and she can return to page one carrying the voice and meaning of the completed story. ([The Daily Beast][7])

That's an excellent lesson for novelists.

You need **an opening** to write the book.

You don't necessarily need **the opening**.

Write something that gets you through the door.

You can build the entrance properly once you know where the house actually leads.

---

# Give yourself a basic map, then allow discovery

Tan isn't entirely a pantser.

She has described making rough plans in journals. She sketches a very basic outline—sometimes only a couple of paragraphs—then adds details, connections, events, and character attributes as they occur to her. ([The Daily Beast][7])

The attached guide likewise encourages mapping major events when outlining, but keeping them concise and focused on the story's central dramatic question. 

So her process is roughly:

**Direction rather than blueprint.**

She knows enough to begin moving.

She doesn't require every discovery to be predetermined.

---

# Choose point of view according to what the story needs

Tan doesn't believe there is one ideal POV.

She particularly likes **first person** for emotional stories because it gives tremendous intimacy. You can put the reader inside the character's body and allow that character to compare current feelings with remembered ones.

But first person restricts access to other minds.

Third-person omniscient gives the writer enormous freedom: motivations, histories, secrets, and perspectives can all become available.

So she recommends choosing POV based partly on:

the kind of story,

the amount of action,

the number of important characters,

the desired emotional intimacy,

and what the central character needs to discover.

And crucially:

**The decision isn't permanent.**

Try something else if it doesn't work. ([MasterClass][8])

The attached guide even recommends rewriting the same story premise in first, second, and third person and watching how the possible story changes. 

That's a terrific exercise before committing to an 80,000-word novel.

---

# Character comes from deeply felt motivation

Tan encourages writers to borrow emotional material from themselves and people they know without simply copying those people.

With *The Joy Luck Club*, for example, fictional circumstances allowed her to explore emotional reactions rooted in herself and her family. ([MasterClass][9])

So when building a character, don't stop at:

> She wants to become a doctor.

Go deeper:

> Why?

Because her father expected it?

Because her sister died?

Because she wants status?

Because she genuinely loves medicine?

Because she has spent her entire life trying to prove she's smarter than her brother?

And then go deeper again.

**Why does that matter?**

Tan's characters become compelling because external circumstances sit on top of complicated emotional histories.

---

# Specific personal truth can become universal

Tan resists the instinct to make a character "universal" by making them generic.

Her work suggests almost the opposite.

Specificity creates connection.

The attached guide's discussion of Chinese and Chinese American writers emphasizes how culturally specific perspectives can illuminate broader experiences without erasing their particular context. 

Tan similarly says that when she reaches a highly specific personal truth in fiction, readers often recognize something of themselves in it. ([PBS][2])

So don't write:

> She felt conflicted about her family traditions.

Write the particular dinner.

The particular food.

The particular insult.

The particular dress.

The particular thing her mother refused to throw away.

**Specificity is what gives universality something to attach itself to.**

---

# Research deeply enough to inhabit the world—but know when to stop

Tan loves research.

That's part of the problem.

Her warning is that writers can research indefinitely because learning about their subject feels productive.

She advises getting the necessary facts right, particularly when recreating another historical period, but warns against disappearing into irrelevant minutiae. ([MasterClass][10])

Her approach also goes beyond conventional factual sources.

If you want to understand the **mindset** of another period, she recommends reading fiction from or about that world—novels of manners, for example—to understand social behavior, values, and assumptions. ([MasterClass][10])

The practical Tan rule is:

**Research until the world becomes usable for storytelling—not until you know everything about it.**

---

# Observe real life constantly

Research doesn't only happen in libraries.

Tan treats observation as part of the writer's work.

The attached guide encourages keeping a journal containing thoughts, emotions, observations, descriptions of places, and even bits of dialogue overheard from other people. Recording how people actually speak can later inform dialogue and plot. 

This gives you a simple daily writing practice:

Become a collector.

Expressions.

Odd sentences.

Arguments.

Gestures.

Rooms.

Objects.

Birds.

Strangers.

Things people misunderstand.

Things people pretend not to notice.

You're building raw material.

---

# Keep a journal

Tan uses journals extensively, both for personal observation and novel development.

The guide recommends allowing the journal to take whatever form keeps you using it: prose, lists, fragments, observations, letters, sketches, even visual journaling. Tan herself uses drawing as a way of forcing herself to slow down and observe details. 

This is important because the journal does **not** need to produce polished prose.

It's a laboratory.

A strange observation written today may become a character five years later.

A line overheard in an airport may become dialogue.

A memory may suddenly connect to another memory.

Tan's planning process also uses journals to draw arrows and connect apparently unrelated ideas. ([The Daily Beast][7])

---

# Objects can unlock stories

The guide recommends using emotionally significant objects as writing prompts.

Take an object—a family heirloom, piece of clothing, photograph, bed, trinket—and ask:

Why does it matter?

When did it acquire meaning?

Has that meaning changed?

Who owned it before?

What would it mean to lose it?

Then freewrite. 

This is very Tan.

She frequently approaches huge emotional subjects through **specific physical details**.

Instead of beginning with "my complicated relationship with my mother," begin with:

**my mother's ring.**

The object gives the abstraction somewhere to live.

---

# Don't censor yourself too early

Tan discovered something important while writing *Where the Past Begins*.

She had a deadline requiring her to produce a substantial amount of material regularly, and she deliberately wrote spontaneously rather than laboriously revising every passage. She found that reducing her self-consciousness allowed more honest material to emerge. ([Poets & Writers][11])

The attached guide offers a similar timed-fiction exercise: answer emotionally provocative questions, give yourself only a minute to begin, then continue for thirty minutes without overthinking prose or structure. 

That's an important distinction in Tan's method:

**Discovery requires freedom.
Revision requires judgment.**

Trying to perform both jobs simultaneously can choke the story.

---

# Choose words deliberately—but accept that perfect exactitude is impossible

Tan loves language.

She talks about searching for words that express something with greater precision while acknowledging that absolute exactitude is impossible.

Her practical advice is to look for words that **do more work**. Notice weak or generic language. Look for opportunities where a more precise word can contribute image, tone, or emotional resonance. ([MasterClass][12])

That doesn't mean decorating every sentence.

It means noticing when a word isn't carrying its share of the load.

---

# Avoid clichés because they belong to somebody else's imagination

Tan's reasoning about clichés is particularly good.

The attached guide explains that clichés are associations someone else has already made. Because readers have encountered them repeatedly, they are both less personal and less powerful. 

Instead of:

> cold as ice

ask yourself what **coldness** reminds *you* of.

Instead of:

> heart pounding like a drum

ask what this character would compare the sensation to.

A mechanic may make one comparison.

A violinist another.

A twelve-year-old another.

A woman remembering childbirth another.

Now metaphor also becomes characterization.

---

# Build metaphors through genuine association

Tan recommends practicing metaphor by learning something unfamiliar from an expert.

Keep asking comparison questions:

Is it like this?

No?

Then what is it like?

The resulting chain of associations teaches you how metaphors actually emerge—not from hunting for "beautiful language," but from attempting to understand one thing through another. 

That's an excellent creative-writing rule:

**Don't ask, "What impressive metaphor can I write?"**

Ask:

> **What does this genuinely remind this consciousness of?**

---

# Let dialogue reveal different inner worlds

Tan is highly attentive to how people actually speak.

But recording speech isn't merely about reproducing realistic dialogue. Her larger interest is in the difference between **what someone experiences internally and what they are willing or able to say externally**.

Her advice on observation—recording conversations and overheard speech—helps writers develop dialogue, while her approach to character voice asks what each individual notices and understands.  ([National Endowment for the Arts][4])

That creates fertile dramatic territory:

**What does the character think?**

versus

**What does the character say?**

The gap between those two things is often where the scene becomes interesting.

---

# Writer's block usually means there is a problem to solve

Tan doesn't interpret writer's block as proof that you're not a writer.

Quite the opposite.

She says it can mean that you've created **too many possibilities**, that you've written yourself into a corner, that the character no longer works, or that something in the narrative has constrained your ability to continue. ([MasterClass][13])

That's a much more useful diagnosis than:

> I have no ideas.

Ask instead:

> **Where did the story become unable to move?**

Maybe the wrong character is narrating.

Maybe you've forced the protagonist toward an event they wouldn't plausibly choose.

Maybe you've decided the ending prematurely.

Maybe you've accumulated five competing versions of the novel.

Writer's block becomes a **story problem**, not a verdict on your talent.

---

# You don't actually have to write every day

Tan jokes about the absolutist command that "real writers write every day."

Her more useful version is:

If you're not physically writing, **keep thinking about the story**. She often thinks through problems before going to sleep. ([MasterClass][13])

This fits her broader process.

Writing isn't limited to typing.

Observing is writing work.

Remembering is writing work.

Researching can be writing work.

Walking while thinking about a character can be writing work.

The important question is whether you're still **engaged with the story**.

---

# Procrastination can sometimes be part of creativity

Tan has an unusually forgiving attitude toward procrastination.

She bird-watches, draws, spends time in nature, and pursues activities that might superficially look like avoidance. But they also cultivate observation, patience, and mental space. The guide presents some forms of procrastination as periods of rumination rather than simple laziness. 

The distinction matters.

Scrolling aimlessly for four hours may not help your novel.

Walking through a neighborhood while unconsciously turning over a story problem might.

Tan's lesson is not:

> Avoid writing.

It's:

> **Creative incubation is real.**

Your conscious mind doesn't have to be hammering the problem every second.

---

# Allow intuition and serendipity into the process

Tan talks about a "muse," "ghost," and "doppelgänger" as imaginative ways of describing the strange moments when intuition delivers something you weren't consciously searching for.

She doesn't insist on a supernatural explanation. The useful part is allowing yourself to recognize moments when seemingly unrelated things unexpectedly connect.

Keep working long enough, she suggests, and sometimes the story suddenly opens up and you understand where you're going. ([MasterClass][14])

This connects with her journaling, research, memory, and observation.

You're constantly putting material into the mind.

Eventually the mind starts making connections you didn't consciously design.

---

# Revision is writing

The attached guide gives this section the wonderfully direct title **"Writing Is Rewriting."** 

And Tan means it.

She says she may revise a page an enormous number of times. The point isn't that writers should literally count to 100; it's that needing repeated revision is completely normal. ([MasterClass][15])

A rough draft isn't evidence that something went wrong.

**The roughness is the material revision works with.**

---

# Be tough on the manuscript, not on yourself

This may be her most valuable revision lesson.

Tan warns writers not to equate criticism of the manuscript with criticism of themselves.

The material has to be personal while you're creating it.

But during revision, you need enough separation to say:

This sentence doesn't work.

This character doesn't work.

This entire subplot doesn't work.

And none of those statements mean:

**I don't work.**

([MasterClass][15])

That psychological separation makes ruthless revision possible.

---

# Revise from large problems to small ones

The attached guide offers a very practical four-stage editing model.

First comes **conceptual editing**: characters, plot, scenes, chapter order, major structural changes.

Then **line editing**: economy, redundancies, weak language, sentence-level precision.

Then **chapter editing**: determine what each chapter actually accomplishes and identify chapters that are overloaded, dull, unnecessary, or misplaced.

Finally comes **gestation**: put the manuscript away and return with fresher eyes. 

That's an excellent order.

Don't spend forty minutes polishing a paragraph in Chapter 19 before deciding whether Chapter 19 needs to exist.

**Structure → chapter → scene → sentence → word.**

Not the reverse.

---

# Make every chapter justify itself

The chapter-edit exercise in the guide is especially useful for novelists.

After finishing the manuscript, summarize each chapter with:

> **What did this chapter accomplish?**

A chapter can legitimately exist to set up something later. But it still needs a purpose.

When you've summarized every chapter, you've effectively created a reverse outline of the book. That makes pacing problems visible: chapters that accomplish too many things, chapters that do almost nothing, repeated beats, and stretches where the novel stalls. 

This is one of the strongest concrete techniques in the whole guide.

---

# Don't over-edit the life out of the book

Tan is a relentless reviser, but she also recognizes the danger of endless refinement.

The guide warns that over-editing can destroy some of the original energy that made the material good in the first place. 

So revision has two obligations:

**Improve what's weak.**

and

**Protect what's alive.**

Those goals sometimes conflict.

---

# Find readers who understand what you're trying to do

Tan values writing groups and trusted writers.

Her Rutgers interview emphasizes finding someone "simpatico" with the kind of writing you want to create—not necessarily someone who rewrites your sentences, but someone capable of asking intelligent questions about your intentions, characters, story, and voice. ([Rutgers University][6])

The attached guide similarly recommends communities where writers regularly exchange constructive criticism and support. 

The important idea is:

**Feedback should help you discover your story—not turn it into somebody else's story.**

---

# Rejection is normal

Tan literally displayed her rejection letters.

The purpose wasn't self-punishment. It was to remove some of their power by confronting them.

She wanted to be capable of saying that even if somebody rejected the work, writing it had meaning for **her**. 

Her MasterClass includes actual rejection letters, including detailed criticisms of stories that ultimately preceded an extraordinarily successful career. Her point is straightforward: every writer gets rejected, including accomplished ones. ([MasterClass][16])

Publication is somebody else's decision.

Continuing to write is yours.

---

# Anxiety isn't evidence that you're doing it wrong

Tan has spoken openly about feeling anxious, blocked, inadequate, and afraid that she cannot write—even after decades of success.

She told the *Los Angeles Times* that these feelings are part of being a writer who wants the work to be good. ([Los Angeles Times][17])

That's important because writers often compare:

**their private experience of uncertainty**

with

**another writer's finished book.**

Those aren't comparable things.

The finished book hides the fear, abandoned drafts, deleted pages, and revisions that created it.

---

# An ending should reveal what the story meant

Tan's philosophy of endings fits everything else she believes.

She often doesn't completely know the ending beforehand.

As she approaches it, the **meaning of the story accumulates** until its direction becomes clear. Sometimes she only sees the path when she's roughly fifty pages away, then suddenly can move quickly toward it. ([MasterClass][18])

That's very different from treating the ending only as plot resolution.

For Tan, the deeper ending answers:

> **What has this experience come to mean?**

---

# A good ending is also a beginning

Tan says many great novel endings are really **new beginnings**.

The character has crossed some threshold of understanding.

Life doesn't stop because the book does.

Perhaps the external situation is different.

Perhaps nothing externally changes but the character now understands it differently.

The endpoint represents transition rather than absolute closure. ([MasterClass][18])

This gives you a useful ending question:

> **What can this character understand on the final page that they could not have understood on page one?**

That is very close to the heart of Tan's storytelling philosophy.

---

# The Amy Tan Writing Checklist

If I reduced her approach to a reusable novel-writing checklist, it would be:

1. **What truth am I curious about rather than already certain about?**
2. **What emotional memory can I draw from—even if I completely change the facts?**
3. **What does this experience actually feel like in the body?**
4. **What contradictions make my character human?**
5. **What does my character believe about how the world works?**
6. **What does this narrator uniquely notice?**
7. **Does the opening establish an interesting consciousness quickly?**
8. **Am I willing to rewrite the opening after I understand the whole novel?**
9. **Which POV gives this story the right combination of intimacy and freedom?**
10. **What does my character want—and what deeper emotional reason makes them want it?**
11. **Am I being specific rather than trying to manufacture universality?**
12. **Have I researched enough to inhabit the world without hiding from writing in research?**
13. **What have I observed in real life that I can save in a journal?**
14. **Can an object, memory, conversation, or image unlock something unexpected?**
15. **Am I allowing myself to discover material before judging it?**
16. **Are my words precise and personal rather than inherited clichés?**
17. **Does this metaphor arise from this narrator's particular consciousness?**
18. **If I'm blocked, what narrative problem have I created?**
19. **What does every chapter accomplish?**
20. **Am I revising the structure before polishing sentences?**
21. **Can I criticize the manuscript without criticizing myself?**
22. **Have I given the manuscript enough distance before another revision?**
23. **Am I protecting the original energy rather than polishing it to death?**
24. **Are my readers helping me understand my intentions instead of rewriting the book for me?**
25. **What has the character—and perhaps I, as the writer—discovered by the ending?**

The deepest Tan principle underneath all of this is something like:

### Don't write fiction merely to demonstrate what you already know.

### Enter the story to discover something.

Memory gives you emotional material. Imagination releases it from literal fact. Character lets you examine contradictory truths. Voice determines how you see them. Revision clarifies what you've found.

And eventually, if the process works, the novel reveals **what it was really about**.

That's the biggest contrast I see between Tan and Sorkin. **Sorkin asks, "Who wants what, and what's stopping them?" Tan asks, "What does this mean, why does it feel this way, and what truth might I discover if I follow it?"** They are extremely complementary approaches: Sorkin gives you the engine that makes the story move; Tan gives you the interior depth that can make the story matter.
