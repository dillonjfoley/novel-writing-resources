I went through the attached *Aaron Sorkin Teaches Screenwriting* workbook and compared it with interviews, Q&As, and other public comments Sorkin has made about his process. The striking thing is how consistent he is: **Sorkin's entire writing philosophy grows out of intention, obstacle, and tactics.** Almost everything else—character, plot, dialogue, scene construction, rewriting—comes after that.

Below is a consolidated version of **Aaron Sorkin's approach to writing**, arranged roughly in the order a writer would actually use it. Although he teaches screenwriting, most of the principles transfer very well to novels.

# Aaron Sorkin's Method of Writing

## Begin with drama, not merely an idea

Sorkin makes a distinction between something that is **interesting** and something that is actually a **story**.

A fascinating person is not necessarily a story. A fascinating world is not necessarily a story. A cool situation is not necessarily a story.

You get a story when somebody wants something **but** something significant prevents them from getting it.

His shorthand test is whether you can describe the idea using a word such as **"but," "except," or "and then."** Those words introduce the complication that turns a premise into drama. 

So:

> A young prince is heir to an empire.

That's a premise.

But:

> A young prince must inherit the empire **but** his father has named his illegitimate brother heir instead.

Now you have drama.

This principle appears over and over in Sorkin's interviews. When WIRED asked him what matters most when constructing plot, world, or character, he returned immediately to intention and obstacle: somebody wants something, and something formidable prevents them from getting it. ([WIRED][1])

Sorkin still described his process exactly this way in a 2026 *Esquire* interview. Before writing, he asks: **Who wants what, and what is stopping them from getting it?** ([Esquire][2])

That is probably the single most important Sorkin rule.

---

# Find the character's intention

The **intention** is what the character is actively trying to accomplish.

Not:

"Sarah wants happiness."

But:

"Sarah wants her husband to admit he cheated."

Not:

"Jon wants acceptance."

But:

"Jon wants to win admission to the king's guard."

Sorkin wants intentions that can produce action.

He argues that the story doesn't really begin until the intention becomes clear. The attached workbook calls intention and obstacle the story's **"drive shaft."** 

And the intention needs to matter badly to the character.

If the audience thinks:

> Why doesn't she just give up?

the story is in trouble.

Sorkin recommends **pressing** the intention. Increase its importance, urgency, or consequences until walking away is extremely difficult. The workbook specifically advises making the stakes high, urgent, convincing, and introducing the central dramatic engine relatively early. 

For a novelist, a useful Sorkin question therefore becomes:

**What does my protagonist want so badly that they will keep acting even when pursuing it becomes painful?**

---

# Make the obstacle formidable

A strong desire isn't sufficient. The thing preventing the character from succeeding needs to deserve an entire story.

Sorkin frequently gives deliberately simple examples:

Someone wants the money.

Someone wants the girl.

Someone needs to reach Philadelphia.

Someone wants to win the game.

The originality isn't necessarily in the desire. Drama comes from how severely the writer prevents the character from satisfying it.

In his MasterClass example of a cross-country road trip, merely driving from New York to Los Angeles doesn't produce much dramatic propulsion. Give one passenger a critical reason to arrive in Los Angeles by a specific time—and then throw delays, breakdowns, weather, mistakes, and other barriers at them—and the anecdotes can suddenly hang from a dramatic spine. ([MasterClass][3])

This suggests a useful revision question:

**Can I make the obstacle worse without making it unbelievable?**

Sorkin's stories often work because the answer keeps being yes.

---

# Character comes from tactics

This is one of Sorkin's most useful ideas for novelists.

He doesn't begin by constructing enormous biographies explaining someone's childhood, favorite foods, personality type, childhood pets, and twenty-seven formative experiences.

Instead:

**Intention + obstacle produce tactics.
Tactics reveal character.**

Two characters can want exactly the same thing but pursue it differently.

Suppose both characters desperately need $10,000.

One begs.

One lies.

One seduces.

One bargains.

One steals.

One threatens.

One manipulates someone's guilt.

One quietly takes a second job.

Those choices tell us who these people are.

The workbook explicitly says that characters emerge from what they want, what prevents them from getting it, and the tactics they choose to overcome those obstacles. Sorkin recommends focusing character development on facts relevant to the conflict rather than writing enormous biographies. 

This gives you an extremely useful character-building formula:

**Want → Resistance → Choice → Character**

Instead of asking:

> What personality traits does this character have?

ask:

> When this person doesn't get what they want, what do they do next?

That is much closer to Sorkin's conception of characterization.

---

# Don't judge your villains

Sorkin is particularly strong on antagonists.

He argues that the writer cannot write a villain while mentally standing outside the character saying, "This person is evil."

The writer needs to **make the antagonist's case**.

His recurring metaphor is that you should be capable of presenting the character's case to God for why they deserve entrance into heaven. In discussions of characters such as Colonel Jessup and Mark Zuckerberg, Sorkin has said that he needs empathy for them even when their behavior is objectionable. ([Groucho Reviews][4])

The workbook gives essentially the same advice: identify with the antihero instead of judging them, or you risk creating a cartoon. 

That doesn't mean excusing them.

It means understanding:

**What do they want?
Why do they believe they're right?
What would they say in their own defense?**

A villain who thinks:

> I'm evil and want power.

is usually weak.

A villain who thinks:

> Someone has to make the decision everyone else is too cowardly to make.

is potentially much more interesting.

---

# Don't start with theme

Sorkin's attitude toward theme is somewhat counterintuitive.

He likes theme, but he doesn't generally use theme to **power the story**.

In an interview discussing his process, he compared theme to something that makes the ride worthwhile, while intention and obstacle are what actually make the car move. ([Screenwriting from Iowa][5])

This suggests:

Don't begin with:

> I want to write a book about grief.

Start with:

> A widowed father wants to stop his daughter from leaving home, but she has secretly accepted a job overseas.

Now grief can emerge through drama.

Sorkin's approach is essentially:

**Story first. Meaning emerges through the struggle.**

---

# Every scene needs intention and obstacle too

Sorkin doesn't reserve this principle for the entire story.

He applies it recursively.

The **book has an intention and obstacle.**

The **act has dramatic movement.**

And the **scene has an intention and obstacle.**

Before writing a scene, ask:

**Who wants something right now?**

Then:

**Who or what prevents them from getting it?**

Then:

**What tactics do they use?**

The workbook's *Steve Jobs* example shows exactly this. Jobs wants the voice demonstration to work. Hertzfeld wants Jobs to understand that it cannot work. Jobs uses intimidation and humiliation; Hertzfeld uses logic. Their incompatible intentions create the scene's conflict. 

Notice how concrete that is.

Not:

> This scene explores Steve's arrogance.

Instead:

> Steve needs Andy to make the computer speak.

The arrogance emerges from **how Steve pursues that objective**.

---

# A scene must change something

Sorkin has another exceptionally practical scene rule:

At the end of the scene, the story should be **at least one step farther ahead**.

Every scene should accomplish something and move the story forward. 

That doesn't mean every scene requires:

a murder,

an explosion,

a revelation,

or a cliffhanger.

But something should be different afterward.

Someone learns something.

Someone decides something.

A relationship changes.

A plan succeeds.

A plan fails.

An obstacle grows.

A new intention forms.

Sorkin even suggests that if you don't know what scene should come next, look at a question raised by the preceding scene and **answer it**. 

That's a very useful novel-writing technique.

Scene A creates a question.

Scene B answers it—but creates another question.

That generates narrative momentum.

---

# Enter scenes late

Sorkin likes dropping the audience into something already happening.

His workbook recommends sometimes beginning a scene in the middle of a conversation so the audience has to catch up. 

That reflects a broader Sorkin principle:

**Don't make the audience wait while everyone gets ready for the scene.**

Instead of:

> John entered the office.
> "Hello."
> "Hello."
> "Sit down."
> "Thank you."
> "Would you like coffee?"
> "No."
> "I suppose you're wondering why I called you."

Try:

> "You stole three million dollars."

Now the reader is running after the story.

Sorkin describes the desired feeling almost like the audience jumping onto a moving train.

---

# Trust the audience—but never confuse them

Sorkin wants an active audience.

The workbook says audiences enjoy receiving clues and assembling meaning themselves. A successful reversal becomes particularly satisfying when they had enough information to participate but still didn't predict it. 

So Sorkin opposes two extremes.

**Over-explanation** insults the audience.

**Confusion** breaks their trust.

The ideal is:

> Give readers everything they need—but don't do their thinking for them.

This is especially relevant to fantasy and science fiction. Worldbuilding information should arrive because the reader **needs it to understand the current drama**, rather than because the author has invented it.

---

# Make exposition dramatic

Sorkin doesn't believe exposition can simply be eliminated. The audience sometimes needs information.

His solution is often to introduce someone who knows approximately what the audience knows.

Courtroom stories work beautifully for him because the jury needs explanations. The lawyers can therefore explain complicated events to the jury while actually explaining them to the audience. He has discussed this technique explicitly when talking about *The Trial of the Chicago 7*. ([Writers Guild of America East][6])

For novels, that doesn't mean creating a clueless character solely to ask questions.

The broader principle is:

**Give exposition a dramatic reason to exist.**

Someone needs the information.

Someone wants to hide the information.

Someone misunderstands the information.

Someone disputes the information.

Someone discovers the information.

Then exposition becomes action.

---

# Plot should run on cause and effect

Sorkin is heavily influenced by Aristotle's *Poetics*.

The workbook's Aristotle appendix stresses unified action: scenes should arise from what came before and lead into what comes after. If removing a scene or line doesn't damage the story, that may indicate it doesn't belong. 

That gives you a powerful Sorkin-style plot test.

Weak plotting often looks like:

**This happens. Then this happens. Then this happens.**

Strong plotting looks like:

**Because this happens, the character does this, which causes this, forcing them to do this...**

The protagonist's choices create consequences.

Consequences create new obstacles.

New obstacles force new tactics.

Those tactics reveal more character.

That is the machinery of Sorkin drama.

---

# Prefer believable impossibility to convenient coincidence

Sorkin borrows Aristotle's principle that a **probable impossibility** is preferable to an **improbable possibility**.

Something extraordinary can work if the audience believes it inside the story.

Something technically possible can fail if it feels absurdly convenient.

The workbook warns against coincidences that exist purely to move the screenplay forward.  

In fantasy, this matters enormously.

A dragon appearing may be impossible in reality but completely believable within the rules of the story.

The hero finding the exact magical sword they require because they randomly opened the correct cupboard at the correct moment may be "possible" inside that world—and still feel like cheating.

---

# Dialogue is music

This is probably Sorkin's second most famous principle.

He doesn't think good dialogue should imitate ordinary conversation.

Ordinary conversation contains repetition, filler, false starts, unfinished sentences, and long stretches where nothing particularly interesting happens.

Drama is compressed reality.

Sorkin thinks dialogue has qualities similar to music:

**rhythm, cadence, pacing, tone, volume, repetition, emphasis, and sound.**

The workbook advises writers to physically say dialogue aloud because dramatic writing is ultimately intended to be performed. 

Sorkin told *Fresh Air* that the sound of the words can matter to him almost as much as their literal meaning. ([Fresh Air Archive][7]) The Library of Congress interview about *The Social Network* similarly has him discussing speech in terms of tone, pitch, and rhythm. ([Library of Congress][8])

For a novelist, the takeaway is not "write everyone like Aaron Sorkin."

It's:

**Read your dialogue aloud.**

Listen for where your mouth trips.

Listen for unnecessary words.

Listen for repetition.

Listen for rhythm.

Listen for the strong word at the end of the sentence.

Dialogue should be **speakable**.

---

# Don't write dialogue until the dramatic machinery works

This is an important qualification because Sorkin is famous for dialogue.

He warns against writing pages of clever conversation without a strong dramatic engine.

Once intention and obstacle are strong, the clever dialogue has something to **do**.

The workbook's *West Wing* discussion says that after the "drive shaft" has been built, the writer gets to enjoy things such as snappy dialogue. 

In other words:

**Clever dialogue is decoration on conflict. It isn't a substitute for conflict.**

That's probably one of the most useful lessons to take from Sorkin precisely because his dialogue is so celebrated.

---

# Research for story, not for trivia

Sorkin distinguishes between factual **nuts-and-bolts research** and research that generates drama.

He recommends looking for discoveries that can produce plot, conflict, scenes, or dialogue rather than endlessly collecting information.

When interviewing people, one of his favored prompts is essentially:

**Tell me something I don't know.**

He also recommends building trust before asking the most difficult questions when multiple interviews are possible. 

For historical fiction or technical fiction, this means research isn't finished when you know **how something works**.

The more valuable question may be:

> What did I discover that creates a story problem?

---

# Sorkin doesn't really outline in the traditional sense

There's an interesting nuance between Sorkin's MasterClass exercises and his personal process.

The workbook recommends index cards and mapping upcoming scenes. 

But Sorkin said in his 2026 *Esquire* interview that he has **never outlined** a screenplay in the conventional sense and often doesn't know the ending when he begins. What he does need is intention and obstacle—and preferably an interesting first scene. Once he gets a scene, he'll put it on an index card and gradually accumulate the story. ([Esquire][2])

So his actual process seems closer to:

**Find the dramatic engine → find a scene → write → discover another scene → card it → continue.**

He is therefore neither a pure planner nor a completely blind discovery writer.

The thing he plans rigorously is **dramatic purpose**.

---

# Don't wait until you know everything

Sorkin says that once he has a strong intention and obstacle for the first scene, he can begin.

He does not necessarily need Scene 2 figured out.

That is useful advice for writers who spend years "preparing" instead of drafting.

You need enough story architecture to keep the prose from becoming aimless.

You don't necessarily need the entire architecture.

---

# When blocked, work on progress rather than perfection

Sorkin openly admits that writing is difficult for him.

He drives, listens to music, thinks for long periods, and can struggle badly with the blank page. The workbook emphasizes giving yourself small milestones and measuring success by **progress**, rather than comparing your productivity with other writers. 

His 2026 description of his process is remarkably similar: getting from page zero to page two can be agonizing, but once he discovers the first scene and dramatic engine, movement becomes easier. ([Esquire][2])

The lesson isn't "wait for inspiration."

It is:

**Find the next solvable dramatic problem.**

---

# Finish before you start fixing

Sorkin strongly recommends reaching the end of the first draft before undertaking major revision.

Why?

Because before you've finished, you're trying to solve two problems simultaneously:

**What is this story?**

and

**How do I make this story good?**

After reaching the ending, you understand the story much better.

Then rewriting becomes problem solving.

The workbook advises getting to the end first, then cutting anything unrelated to the main conflict. 

Sorkin repeated this approach in interviews: once he's reached the ending, he can return to early material, eliminate dead ends, and strengthen material that later became important. ([Script Magazine][9])

This is especially useful advice for novels.

**Draft one discovers the book.
Draft two makes the book look intentional.**

---

# Rewrite toward the central conflict

Sorkin compares a first draft to a block of marble.

Revision involves removing what isn't part of the statue.

His test is ruthless:

Does this scene contribute to the primary drama?

Does this speech?

Does this joke?

Does this subplot?

Does this clever exchange?

If not, it may have to go—even if it's one of your favorite pieces.

That's his version of **kill your darlings**. 

Sorkin sometimes goes so far as to **retype the entire screenplay**, including versions reconstructed from memory, because it forces him to reconsider every word and naturally discard weaker material. 

That's extreme for a novelist, but the underlying technique is excellent:

Don't merely proofread a draft.

**Reconsider it.**

---

# Listen to the problem behind a note

Sorkin's approach to criticism is sophisticated.

A reader may correctly sense that something is wrong while being completely wrong about how you should fix it.

So if someone says:

> The protagonist should have a brother.

the important information may not be "add a brother."

The important information may be:

> I'm not emotionally invested in the protagonist.

The writer owns the solution.

The workbook advises finding readers who understand both scripts and your particular writing, asking for specific notes, and treating even imperfect feedback as possible evidence of an underlying problem. 

That is an excellent rule for novel feedback as well.

---

# Don't make characters perform the audience's emotion

One of Sorkin's recurring revision notes is that a scene can become **"too wet."**

That happens when the characters visibly perform the exact emotion the writer wants the audience to feel. 

If you want the reader to cry, having everyone sob and explain how tragic the moment is may weaken it.

The stronger scene may be restrained.

Let the **audience experience the emotion** rather than commanding the characters to experience it on their behalf.

---

# Study writing diagnostically

Sorkin doesn't recommend merely consuming great movies and plays.

He recommends **taking them apart**.

Read scripts while watching films. Ask why scenes work. Ask why bad scenes don't work. Study intention, obstacle, scene movement, exposition, reversals, pacing, and structure.

The workbook explicitly tells writers to become "diagnosticians" and learn dramatic principles by deconstructing stories. 

This may be one of the best ways to improve quickly.

Instead of:

> I loved that movie.

ask:

> Why was I desperate to know what happened next?

Then find the mechanism.

---

# Write like yourself

For all of his emphasis on craft rules, Sorkin doesn't recommend becoming a Sorkin imitator.

He encourages writers to write what they enjoy and **write like themselves**. 

His dialogue works because musical language is part of *his* natural writing impulse.

Your equivalent might be:

atmosphere,

action,

romance,

humor,

description,

horror,

mystery,

or interiority.

Learn the machinery of drama.

Then run **your voice** through it.

---

# The Sorkin Writing Checklist

Before or during a draft, his philosophy can be reduced to these questions:

1. **What does the protagonist want?**
2. **Why do they need it badly?**
3. **What formidable thing prevents them from getting it?**
4. **What tactics will they use to overcome that obstacle?**
5. **What do those tactics reveal about their character?**
6. **What does the antagonist want, and why do they believe they are right?**
7. **Does every important character want something?**
8. **Does every scene contain an active intention?**
9. **Is there resistance to that intention?**
10. **Is the story farther ahead when the scene ends?**
11. **Does one event cause the next rather than merely precede it?**
12. **Am I trusting the reader without confusing them?**
13. **Is exposition arriving because somebody needs it?**
14. **Does the dialogue sound good aloud?**
15. **Am I writing clever dialogue instead of drama?**
16. **Have I made the stakes and obstacles difficult enough?**
17. **Am I allowing the audience to feel emotion rather than forcing characters to demonstrate it?**
18. **Can I remove this scene, subplot, description, or conversation without hurting the central story?**
19. **Am I fixing the actual problem behind feedback instead of blindly following the suggested solution?**
20. **Am I making progress rather than waiting for perfection?**

The central Sorkin idea underneath all twenty questions is remarkably simple:

### Somebody wants something.

### Something formidable prevents them from getting it.

### What they do about that problem creates the character and the story.

Everything else is craftsmanship applied to that engine.

And one thing I found particularly useful after comparing the MasterClass workbook with his interviews is that **Sorkin has not really changed this philosophy over decades**. In a recent 2026 interview he is still describing his starting point almost exactly the same way he did in older interviews and in the MasterClass: find the intention and obstacle first. ([Esquire][2])

For the kind of novels you've been developing, I think the next useful step would be to turn this into an **"Aaron Sorkin Novel Writing Method"**—translating these screenwriting principles specifically into **novel outlining, chapters, character arcs, scenes, dialogue, and revision**, with a reusable worksheet you could apply to each book.
