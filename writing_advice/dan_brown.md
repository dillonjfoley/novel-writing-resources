I went through the attached *Dan Brown Teaches Writing Thrillers* workbook and supplemented it with Brown's public MasterClass material and interviews, including his 2025 discussions around *The Secret of Secrets*. Those newer interviews are especially useful because they show that many of the principles in the older MasterClass are still central to the way he works: **he still outlines heavily, knows his ending before drafting, writes every day, researches far more than he uses, and ruthlessly removes material that does not serve the reader.**

Of all the writers we've looked at so far, Brown may be the most relentlessly **reader-focused**. His central question is rarely "Is this beautiful?" or even "Is this realistic?" It is closer to:

> **What does the reader want to know right now, and how do I make them need to turn one more page to find out?**

# Dan Brown's Method of Writing

## The writer's first job is to hold attention

This is the foundation of Brown's entire approach.

The attached workbook describes storytelling's central ambition as capturing and sustaining human attention. Suspense, character, research, plot, action, exposition, and prose are all tools serving that larger goal. 

That is worth emphasizing because Brown doesn't treat suspense as something belonging exclusively to thriller novels.

A romance can create suspense:

> Will they get together?

A fantasy:

> Can she defeat the king?

A mystery:

> Who killed him?

A family drama:

> Will the father reconcile with his son?

Brown's craft principles work whenever a reader **wants an answer they do not yet possess**.

---

# Suspense is a promise

This may be Brown's most important storytelling principle.

The moment you raise a question, you have implicitly told the reader:

> Keep reading and eventually I will answer this.

Brown calls that a **contract**.

The attached workbook says novels contain hundreds of these contracts, from the enormous central question to small passing promises. The writer must eventually pay them off or deliberately explain why the expected event didn't occur. 

Suppose you mention on page 30:

> The protagonist has never opened the locked drawer in her father's study.

You've made a promise.

The reader now assumes:

**That drawer matters.**

If the novel ends and the drawer never appears again, you've probably violated the contract.

Brown therefore wants writers constantly tracking:

**What questions have I raised?**

**Which ones have I answered?**

**Which ones am I deliberately withholding?**

---

# Brown's Three C's: Contract, Clock, Crucible

Brown reduces suspense to three foundational components.

### Contract

The question or promise that makes the reader want an answer.

### Clock

Some form of time pressure that means the character cannot deal with the problem whenever convenient.

### Crucible

A situation that traps the characters inside escalating difficulty and prevents them from simply walking away.

The workbook says the crucible should funnel the protagonist through increasingly difficult problems, each harder than what preceded it. If the journey begins getting easier, reader interest usually declines. 

Put together:

**Contract:** Can Sarah rescue her missing daughter?

**Clock:** Her kidnapper says she dies at midnight.

**Crucible:** Sarah cannot involve the police, cannot leave the city, and must perform increasingly dangerous tasks to receive the next clue.

Now you have a story engine.

---

# Your stakes don't have to be global

Brown writes world-threatening conspiracies, but he explicitly says that isn't what makes stakes "high."

Stakes are high when they are high **for the protagonist**.

The hero might be trying to:

save the world,

save a marriage,

keep custody of a child,

avoid bankruptcy,

protect a reputation,

or save a restaurant.

The attached workbook stresses that the reader needs to understand what matters deeply to the protagonist and what losing it would mean. 

So don't ask:

> Is this big enough?

Ask:

> **Is this important enough to this person?**

---

# Find the sole dramatic question

Brown likes reducing a complicated novel to one extremely simple question.

He calls this the **sole dramatic question**.

For *Inferno*, the workbook frames it essentially as:

> Will Robert Langdon find the virus and save the world?

Everything else can become complicated.

Multiple villains.

Conspiracies.

Subplots.

Historical information.

Romance.

Betrayals.

But underneath it is one clean question whose answer the book promises to deliver. 

This is one of Brown's best outlining tools.

For any novel, try completing:

> **Will [protagonist] accomplish [primary objective]?**

If you cannot identify that question, the story may not yet have a strong enough spine.

---

# Keep the sole dramatic question visible while writing

Brown's workbook actually recommends writing this question down and keeping it near your writing desk. 

Why?

Because a complicated book generates seductive detours.

An interesting side character.

A fascinating historical anecdote.

A subplot.

A wonderful setting.

A new relationship.

Whenever the novel starts wandering, return to:

> **What promise did I originally make the reader?**

That's your compass.

---

# Choose a subject you're genuinely fascinated by

Brown rejects the narrow interpretation of "write what you know."

His preferred question is:

> **What do I want to know?**

He argues that curiosity about something unfamiliar may generate more energy than writing only about things you've already mastered. The attached workbook says choosing a world that genuinely interests you can generate characters, locations, research, conflict, and plot. 

That's particularly important because writing a novel takes so long.

Brown's basic test is:

> **Am I curious enough about this to remain excited after hundreds or thousands of hours?**

---

# Start with the world

Brown often develops the **world of the novel before the plot**.

Not necessarily fantasy-style worldbuilding.

"World" can mean:

cryptography,

the Vatican,

art history,

artificial intelligence,

noetics,

Washington politics,

NASA,

a courtroom,

professional ballet,

deep-sea exploration.

Once you select the world, it suggests:

who belongs there,

who doesn't,

what they want,

what they know,

what dangers exist,

what arguments matter,

and what locations might create interesting scenes.

Research starts generating story.

---

# Find a moral gray area inside that world

This is another major Brown principle.

He looks for an intellectually interesting question where reasonable people can disagree.

For *Digital Fortress*, the workbook uses privacy versus national security:

> Is violating individual privacy justified if doing so prevents terrorism?

That isn't simply:

**good versus evil.**

It allows characters on opposing sides to both possess legitimate arguments. 

Brown calls moral gray areas "heavy lifters" because one dilemma can simultaneously generate:

theme,

character motivation,

villain motivation,

dialogue,

conflict,

and plot.

That's efficient storytelling.

---

# Think like a philosopher, then write like a thriller writer

Brown likes enormous questions:

Where did humanity come from?

Where are we going?

What is consciousness?

What happens after death?

Can science and religion coexist?

What is privacy worth?

But the philosophical question cannot remain abstract.

His newest novel illustrates this particularly well. In 2025 Brown explained that his challenge with consciousness was figuring out how to turn something abstract and ethereal into a **concrete, urgent modern thriller**. ([Los Angeles Times][1])

That's an excellent Brown formula:

**Big intellectual question + immediate human crisis.**

Not:

> A professor debates consciousness for 400 pages.

But:

> A discovery about consciousness threatens powerful people, somebody disappears, a manuscript is stolen, and the protagonist has only hours to discover why.

Now philosophy becomes drama.

---

# Don't obsess about whether the premise has been done before

Brown's answer is:

It probably has.

He argues that the **"how" matters more than the "what."**

James Bond repeatedly receives missions, encounters danger, faces villains, and prevails.

The basic structure is not radically reinvented each time.

What changes is:

the villain,

locations,

set pieces,

characters,

specific problems,

tone,

research,

and execution.

The workbook says your particular **how** is what makes an old fundamental story unique. 

That's an excellent antidote to:

> "Someone already wrote something like this."

Of course they did.

The real question is:

> **What is my version?**

---

# Combine interests that don't obviously belong together

Brown's signature fiction often comes from collisions.

Ancient history + modern technology.

Religion + science.

Art + cryptography.

Architecture + murder mystery.

His workbook explicitly encourages writers to trust apparently unrelated interests and look for unexpected connections among them. 

Those intersections are often where originality lives.

Instead of seeking one extraordinary subject:

> **Find two ordinary interests that rarely occupy the same novel.**

---

# Know your ending before writing the thriller

This is perhaps where Brown differs most dramatically from Baldacci.

Brown believes strongly that a thriller writer should know the ending.

The attached workbook recommends writing the finale early because knowing the destination helps prevent the notorious **"middle muddle."** 

And he has become even more emphatic about this.

In a 2025 PBS interview about *The Secret of Secrets*, Brown said that a thriller writer should know the ending before beginning: who the villain is, the ultimate twist, and the basic resolution. The middle can change radically, but the foundation should be known. ([PBS][2])

In another 2025 interview, he described himself simply as **"absolutely a plotter."** ([Audible.com][3])

So Brown's method is much more architect-like than Baldacci's.

---

# Write the beginning and ending, then build the bridge

Brown's recent explanation is fascinating.

He likes establishing the beginning and ending and putting them as far apart as possible **dramatically**, while often compressing them as closely as possible **chronologically**.

If the protagonist's world looks one way at 8:00 a.m. and completely different at midnight, you need a tremendous amount of transformation between those points.

That produces pressure.

Brown described this process in 2025 as knowing Point A and Point B and then putting every imaginable obstacle between them. ([Audible.com][3])

In other words:

**Beginning = current reality.**

**Ending = transformed reality.**

**Plot = everything necessary to make that transformation happen.**

---

# Outline the major events in one sentence each

Brown's workbook recommends an extremely practical method.

Write each main event as a **single short sentence**.

For example:

> Danny gets shot in the leg.

> Sophie discovers the key.

> Langdon reaches the cathedral.

Put these on index cards if useful so they can be physically rearranged.

The objective is not beautiful prose.

It is seeing the **architecture of the book at once**. 

That allows you to spot:

dead sections,

repetition,

missing escalation,

imbalanced subplots,

and promises that never receive payoffs.

---

# Think of the outline as scaffolding

Brown's 2025 comments refine this.

He describes his plot organization as **scaffolding**: he doesn't need every detail simultaneously present in his conscious mind. He needs a framework sturdy enough to hold the details as he develops them. ([PBS][2])

That's useful because outlining can become overwhelming if you imagine you must understand all 100 chapters at once.

Instead:

**Build enough framework to know where the structure goes.**

Then fill sections as you work.

---

# But the outline is allowed to change

Despite being an aggressive plotter, Brown isn't rigid.

The attached workbook explicitly says that characters can disrupt even detailed plans. When that happens, trust the story rather than blindly obeying the original outline. 

So:

**Know the destination.**

But allow yourself to discover a better road.

That's Brown's balance between plotting and creativity.

---

# Build the middle by making everything worse

Brown's solution to the "middle muddle" is not primarily:

> Invent more stuff.

It is:

> **Increase pressure on what already matters.**

The workbook recommends:

introducing new problems;

creating additional obstacles;

complicating existing relationships;

turning intellectual conflict into physical danger;

revealing that the problem is larger than anticipated;

creating secondary-character tensions;

and repeatedly reminding the reader of the stakes. 

The hero's trajectory should generally be:

**hard → harder → worse → almost impossible.**

---

# Paint the hero into a corner

Brown sometimes deliberately creates obstacles **before he knows the solution**.

Why?

Because solving the problem forces him to become more imaginative.

The workbook advises putting the character into an impossible position and seeing what they do. 

That's a useful technique even for planners:

> Don't always design the escape before designing the trap.

Create an intellectually honest impossible situation.

Then make yourself solve it.

---

# The villain creates the hero's story

Brown recommends developing the antagonist early—sometimes **before the protagonist**.

His logic is straightforward:

The villain's actions create the crisis.

The crisis forces the hero to respond.

The hero's response becomes the story.



This gives you a useful thriller-building sequence:

**What does the villain want?**

↓

**What does the villain do to get it?**

↓

**Whose life does that action disrupt?**

↓

**Why is that person uniquely equipped—or uniquely unequipped—to stop them?**

Now you have a protagonist.

---

# Every villain needs their own morality

Brown strongly resists villains who simply think:

> I'm evil.

The villain should believe that their actions are somehow justified.

The workbook puts this in terms of people choosing what they perceive as **their own good**, even when that choice looks monstrous from outside. 

So don't merely ask:

> Why does my villain want power?

Ask:

> **What does my villain believe will happen if they *don't* do this?**

That often produces much stronger motivation.

---

# Your villain can be easier to understand than your hero

Brown wants antagonists who may be morally wrong but intellectually understandable.

Bertrand Zobrist in *Inferno*, for example, believes overpopulation will destroy humanity. His proposed solution is horrifying, but he sees himself as addressing a real problem.

That's much more compelling than:

> He wants everyone dead because he's crazy.

The stronger antagonist may force readers to admit:

> I disagree with the solution—but the problem he's describing is real.

---

# Make the hero flawed

Brown says perfect heroes are boring.

A hero's internal weakness can create as many obstacles as the villain does. 

Robert Langdon's claustrophobia is a classic Brown example.

It's not merely biographical decoration.

Put him underground.

In an elevator.

In a confined chamber.

Suddenly his character trait becomes a **plot obstacle**.

That's efficient characterization.

A useful Brown question:

> **What flaw can I put directly in conflict with what this character will eventually have to do?**

---

# Give the protagonist personal stakes

Even when the external stakes concern millions of lives, Brown wants the conflict to matter personally.

Otherwise the protagonist becomes merely a delivery system for the plot.

What does the hero personally fear losing?

Who do they care about?

What belief might collapse?

What failure would haunt them?

That emotional connection lets large abstract threats become human.

---

# Ask the worst thing that could happen—then make it worse

Brown's character exercise is simple:

> What is the worst thing that could happen to my hero?

Then:

> **How can I make it worse?**



This sounds almost comically cruel.

But it points toward a fundamental thriller principle:

Characters become interesting when their resources disappear.

Take away:

the weapon;

the ally;

the evidence;

the authority;

the phone;

the escape route;

the person's trust;

the time.

Then see what remains.

---

# Let plot generate characters

Brown doesn't believe you need twenty complete biographies before writing.

His workbook says supporting characters frequently emerge because the plot creates a need.

Need someone capable of trapping Langdon in the Louvre?

Create Bezu Fache.

Need someone who can interpret information Langdon cannot?

Create Sophie.

Need someone capable of explaining the conspiracy?

Create Teabing.



Brown recently explains this idea similarly in his public MasterClass material: don't populate a novel with characters and then search for jobs for them. Let the plot tell you who needs to exist. ([MasterClass][4])

---

# Every character needs a function

Brown recommends asking:

> **What is this character's purpose?**

Maybe they:

create danger;

provide expertise;

challenge the protagonist's beliefs;

supply information;

mislead the reader;

create romance;

create humor;

serve as mentor;

or initiate the plot.

If their function is weak or duplicated by somebody else, consider combining or eliminating characters. 

This is especially useful in complex thrillers where casts can become unwieldy.

---

# Keep the cast manageable

Brown advises limiting characters to those who matter.

Readers have limited memory.

If you introduce eleven agents, five cardinals, eight professors, four assassins, and six government officials, you may know who they are.

The reader may not.

For recurring characters, give them a memorable hook:

role,

physical characteristic,

personality,

voice,

relationship,

or behavior.



---

# Give sidekicks skills the hero lacks

Brown likes complementary partners.

The sidekick can:

solve a problem the hero cannot;

provide a contrasting worldview;

challenge the hero;

create emotional intimacy;

become endangered;

or become a romantic possibility.

The attached workbook recommends **oppositional sidekicks** in particular because disagreement creates conversation, thematic complexity, and tension. 

So don't automatically create:

> Hero + person who agrees with hero.

Try:

> Hero + equally intelligent person who sees the world differently.

That's dramatically richer.

---

# Romantic possibility is free tension

Brown points out that simply putting two characters together who **might** become romantically involved creates an ongoing unresolved question:

> Will they?

You don't even necessarily need to answer yes.

The possibility itself produces tension. ([MasterClass][4])

This is Brown's philosophy of promises applied to relationships.

---

# Characters need to want something

Brown doesn't require every supporting character's entire childhood history before they appear.

But he does require one essential piece of information:

> **What do they want?**

That desire tells you how they behave.

The workbook says you don't necessarily need to tell readers the answer, but the writer should know it because desire determines action. 

This connects Brown surprisingly closely to Sorkin.

---

# Create suspense by controlling information

Brown's practical definition of suspense is **information management**.

Who knows what?

When do they know it?

What does the reader know?

What does the protagonist know?

What does the villain know?

What information is missing?

When will it become available?

The workbook calls suspense a series of incremental information exchanges that collectively drive the sole dramatic question. 

That is much more precise than simply saying:

> Make it exciting.

---

# There are two basic suspense modes

Brown distinguishes between:

### Tell the reader more than the hero knows

This creates **dramatic irony**.

The reader sees the assassin waiting upstairs.

The protagonist walks up the stairs unaware.

The question becomes:

> When will she realize?

### Let the reader know only what the hero knows

This creates **mystery and curiosity**.

The protagonist finds blood.

Neither hero nor reader knows whose it is.

The question becomes:

> What happened?

The workbook says both modes can exist inside the same novel and even alternate from scene to scene. 

---

# Withholding is one of Brown's primary tools

Brown argues that readers become participants when information is withheld correctly.

They theorize.

Predict.

Combine evidence.

Ask questions.

This mental activity increases engagement.

But the withholding has to be **plausible**.

The character genuinely cannot know the information.

The reader is limited to a particular POV.

A document is missing.

Someone is lying.

The connection hasn't yet been discovered.

Artificial withholding makes readers feel manipulated. 

---

# Don't confuse withholding with confusion

Brown wants readers **slightly behind**, not completely lost.

A chapter can open with:

> "Don't touch that!"

The reader wonders:

Who said that?

What's happening?

Good.

But the narrative should rapidly establish enough context that curiosity becomes understanding.

The workbook explicitly recommends mild disorientation while warning that clarification must follow. 

The distinction is:

**Curiosity asks, "What is happening?" with confidence an answer is coming.**

**Confusion asks, "What is happening?" because the storytelling has failed.**

---

# Make promises early

Brown likes creating a lot of questions near the beginning.

Then he starts paying them off.

But—and this is the crucial part—**as one promise is fulfilled, another appears**.

The workbook describes his stories as a patchwork of overlapping promises. 

This creates the page-turner rhythm:

**Question A**

→ answer A while introducing question B

→ answer B while worsening question C

→ partial answer C while introducing D

→ reveal that answer A meant something different

→ etc.

The reader never reaches a clean psychological stopping point.

---

# Put a promise in almost every chapter

Brown suggests that nearly anything can function as a promise:

What's in the box?

Who sent the photograph?

Why is the professor lying?

Who planted the bomb?

What does the symbol mean?

Can they escape?

Who is watching them?



It doesn't have to be enormous.

A **micro-question** can pull someone through five pages.

A **macro-question** can pull them through five hundred.

---

# Compress the timeline

This is one of Brown's strongest thriller tools.

If a story takes two weeks:

Could it happen in one?

If it happens over three days:

Could it happen in twenty-four hours?

His novels frequently use extremely compressed timelines because less time means fewer opportunities to:

rest,

research,

recover,

call for help,

reconsider,

or escape.

The workbook explicitly recommends compressing time when a story lacks tension. 

And his 2025 Audible interview makes the same point: constriction makes a book harder to write but more exciting to read. ([Audible.com][3])

---

# Action is not suspense

This is one of Brown's most useful corrections.

**Action is often the payoff to suspense.**

Suspense happens beforehand.

The bomb explodes:

action.

The reader knows a bomb is beneath the table while two characters casually talk:

suspense.

The workbook explicitly says action is not the primary tool of suspense; anticipation created by promises is. 

That distinction can radically improve thrillers.

Don't ask:

> How can I add another action scene?

Ask:

> **How can I make the reader desperately anticipate the next action scene?**

---

# Use "pulses" to keep danger alive

Brown has a particularly useful micro-suspense device called a **pulse**.

Suppose characters are hiding from police.

They spend three pages discussing the clue.

The scene risks becoming calm.

So:

> The phone buzzed again.

or:

> Footsteps passed outside the door.

The danger hasn't actually progressed.

But you've reminded the reader:

**The threat is still here.**

The workbook calls these short reminders "pulses." 

They're particularly useful inside exposition-heavy scenes.

---

# Cliffhangers exploit the desire for closure

Brown frequently ends chapters immediately **before** the answer.

The door opens—

chapter break.

He reads the name—

chapter break.

Someone fires—

chapter break.

The villain removes the mask—

chapter break.

The workbook recommends stopping at the moment preceding fulfillment, when curiosity is strongest. 

And sometimes you don't return to that storyline immediately.

Switch POV.

Now the reader has to keep going through another thread before receiving the answer.

---

# The best cliffhanger sometimes comes from deleting the ending

Brown gives a wonderfully simple editing exercise.

Take an existing scene.

Cut the final few paragraphs.

Move them to the next chapter.

See what happens.

Often the scene suddenly ends on the exact point where the reader most wants resolution. 

That's an excellent practical trick.

---

# Cold-open scenes

Brown recommends entering scenes **late**.

Don't always begin:

> Langdon entered the building, crossed the lobby, took the elevator, walked down the corridor...

Start:

> "There's a body in the elevator."

Then establish context while the scene moves.

Brown's workbook specifically recommends opening at a critical point in conversation or action and weaving contextual information in afterward. 

This is very similar to Sorkin's moving-train principle.

---

# Red herrings must be fair

Brown loves misdirection.

But he says two important things about red herrings.

First, don't obtain a twist by simply refusing to tell readers something the POV character obviously knows.

Second, the false conclusion should grow naturally from actual events.



The ideal twist creates:

> **Of course! How did I not see that?**

Not:

> How could I possibly have known that?

---

# Plant the solution in plain sight

Brown's 2025 discussion of puzzles and twists is particularly useful.

He wants readers to participate.

Ideally they solve the puzzle at the same moment as the protagonist—or see the answer and realize the evidence was sitting in front of them all along. The twist should neither be obvious hours beforehand nor incomprehensible when revealed. ([Audible.com][3])

That's the sweet spot:

**surprising + inevitable in retrospect.**

---

# Start every chapter with a goal

Brown gives each chapter a simple purpose, often written as a single bullet point.

For example:

**Langdon meets Sophie.**

**The assassin enters the church.**

**Reveal why the artifact matters.**

**Hero escapes the police.**

Then he asks:

> **How can I make that interesting?**

The workbook describes this directly. 

This separates two writing problems:

**What must the chapter accomplish?**

and

**What is the most entertaining way to accomplish it?**

---

# The reader should learn something new in every chapter

Brown's rule of thumb is that each opening should provide novelty:

new information,

a new character,

a new setting,

a new problem,

or a new perspective.



This helps prevent what might be called "holding-pattern chapters"—chapters where everybody discusses what they already know without meaningfully changing anything.

---

# Every scene should both move the story and serve a promise

The workbook offers a brutal scene test:

**Does this move the story forward?**

**Does it create or fulfill a contract with the reader?**

If not, change or remove it. 

That's an excellent Brown-style revision rule.

---

# Use "negative space"

Brown does not believe every chapter should operate at maximum intensity.

He borrows from music.

You need:

loud and quiet,

tension and release,

action and reflection.

He calls quieter material **negative space**.

These are opportunities for:

relationships,

internal thought,

backstory,

emotion,

reflection,

and orientation.



Without quiet scenes, loud scenes lose contrast.

---

# Think of a novel like music

Brown began as a musician, and music strongly shapes how he understands narrative.

A piece of music builds expectation and then resolves it.

So does a story.

A musical crescendo becomes increasing tension.

A quiet passage becomes recovery.

A return to the tonic resembles the ending fulfilling the story's original central promise.



That's an excellent way to think about pacing:

**Tension → release → tension → greater tension → release → climax.**

A page-turner isn't one sustained scream.

It's rhythm.

---

# Use scene for important moments and summary for the boring travel between them

Brown distinguishes between fully dramatized **scene** and summarized dramatic narration.

Scene tends to feel faster and immediate.

Summary compresses time.

His workbook recommends dramatizing what's interesting or necessary and moving quickly over less important material. 

You don't need:

three pages of driving to the airport.

You can say:

> Forty minutes later, Langdon stepped into the terminal.

Then dramatize what actually matters.

---

# Point of view is a suspense tool

Brown doesn't treat POV solely as a stylistic choice.

It's an information-control mechanism.

POV decides:

what the reader sees,

what they cannot see,

what they misunderstand,

and which secrets remain inaccessible.

The workbook explicitly describes point of view as useful for withholding information. 

That's a very thriller-specific way of thinking about POV.

---

# Stay inside one point of view during a scene

Brown often uses multiple POV characters.

But once a section establishes a viewpoint, he recommends staying there until a scene or chapter break.

Jumping into another person's mind without warning can break immersion and undermine information control. 

Instead:

Langdon chapter.

Villain chapter.

Sophie chapter.

Back to Langdon.

Now every change in viewpoint becomes structurally useful.

---

# Change POV to increase suspense

Imagine:

The protagonist thinks the house is empty.

End chapter.

Switch POV.

The villain is already in the basement.

Now the reader knows more than the hero.

You've transformed:

**mystery**

into

**dread**.

Brown deliberately uses perspective to alternate these forms of suspense. 

---

# Research is part of idea generation

Brown doesn't treat research as fact-checking performed after the story exists.

Research **creates the story**.

The workbook says research can generate:

the world,

characters,

moral dilemmas,

locations,

and even the main conflict. 

Early research should therefore be exploratory.

Follow curiosity.

Read strange things.

Watch documentaries.

Travel.

Talk to people.

Notice connections.

Structure can come later.

---

# Talk to experts

Books aren't sufficient for Brown.

He recommends speaking directly with specialists because they provide:

details unavailable in books,

personal enthusiasm,

unexpected connections,

real speech,

and potential character inspiration.

But do your homework **before** the interview so you aren't wasting the expert's time asking easily researched questions. 

Then ask deeper questions.

---

# Visit the real place

Brown's location research method is particularly interesting.

On the **first visit**, he may deliberately avoid taking notes or photographs.

He simply experiences the space.

What does it feel like?

What draws his eye?

What's unexpected?

Then he returns with notebooks and cameras to document what he needs. 

That two-stage method separates:

**emotional perception**

from

**data collection**.

Very useful for novelists.

---

# Treat location like a character

Brown believes locations should have:

mood,

identity,

revelations,

and dramatic function.

He sometimes even allows a location to solve a plot problem. A dangerous staircase may suggest a death. A hidden corridor may suggest an escape. A landmark may suggest a clue. 

Don't merely ask:

> Where does this scene happen?

Ask:

> **What can happen here that couldn't happen anywhere else?**

That's how setting becomes story.

---

# Let characters experience setting rather than lecturing about it

Brown warns against stopping the novel so the author can deliver a travel brochure.

Instead of:

> The cathedral was built in 1247 in the Gothic style...

have the character:

touch the cold stone,

hear footsteps echo,

notice the impossible height,

smell incense,

search for an escape route.

The workbook recommends filtering world information through sensory experience and action. 

---

# Research far more than you'll use

Brown says roughly **90 percent** of his research may never appear in the final book.

That's fine.

The unused knowledge gives him confidence and lets him choose the right ten percent. 

His 2025 interviews reinforce the scale of this process: *The Secret of Secrets* required years of research into subjects including consciousness, philosophy, physics, and noetics. ([AP News][5])

---

# If it doesn't serve the story, don't use it

This may be Brown's most important research-editing principle.

You may have spent three weeks learning it.

Doesn't matter.

If the reader doesn't need it, cut it.

The workbook explicitly describes research as money in the bank: use what the current story needs and save the rest. 

Brown said virtually the same thing in 2025: respecting the reader's intelligence and time means putting only what they need onto the page and removing fascinating material that doesn't serve this particular story. ([Audible.com][3])

---

# Don't let research become procrastination

Because Brown genuinely loves research, he recognizes its danger.

At some point:

**Stop learning and begin writing.**

You do not need everything.

You need enough to start.

Research can continue as the manuscript reveals additional questions. 

---

# Keep the prose transparent

Brown is not trying to make readers stop and admire every sentence.

His preferred thriller prose is generally clear enough that the language becomes almost invisible and the reader remains inside the story.

The workbook recommends:

precision,

shorter words where possible,

cutting clichés,

minimizing unnecessary adjectives and adverbs,

reducing passive constructions,

and choosing concrete language. 

This isn't a claim that every novelist must write minimally.

It's Brown's particular priority:

> **Don't let the sentence interrupt the movie playing in the reader's head.**

---

# Cut unnecessary words

The workbook provides an editing exercise that asks writers to remove:

superfluous adjectives;

superfluous adverbs;

passive voice;

generic wording;

and anything that can become more concise. 

Brown's prose philosophy favors speed and clarity.

---

# Exposition should happen through experience

Brown writes research-heavy books, which means he constantly faces an exposition problem.

His preferred solution is to put information inside:

sensory experience,

argument,

dialogue,

movement,

opinion,

or discovery.

The workbook explicitly recommends presenting dry material through the character's perspective rather than delivering detached explanation whenever possible. 

Instead of:

> Here's a page explaining the disputed history of this church.

Try:

> Two scholars argue over what the church means while desperately searching it for a clue.

Now information and conflict happen simultaneously.

---

# Dialogue comes from desire

Brown asks the same basic question Sorkin does:

> What does this character want?

That desire determines how they speak.

Do they:

persuade?

lie?

intimidate?

seduce?

hide?

deflect?

confess?

The workbook specifically connects good dialogue with character desire and recommends creating disagreement between characters rather than conversations in which everyone simply exchanges information. 

---

# Dialogue should sound like different instruments

Again drawing on music, Brown thinks each character should bring a distinctive "sound" to the scene.

A scientist.

A priest.

A police chief.

A professor.

A billionaire.

A frightened child.

They shouldn't all express themselves identically.

But their voices also need to work together dramatically—like different instruments in one composition. 

---

# Put characters in motion while they talk

Whenever possible, don't have characters sit motionless for five pages exchanging information.

Have them:

walk through the museum;

search a room;

drive somewhere;

examine an artifact;

avoid surveillance;

prepare equipment.

Then dialogue, action, exposition, and setting can all perform work simultaneously.

That's another Brown **heavy lifter**—one technique doing several jobs.

---

# Keep dialogue tags invisible

Brown prefers simple tags such as:

> he said

> she said

rather than elaborate attribution full of adverbs.

The goal again is transparency: attribution should not distract from the actual conversation. 

---

# Write much more than readers will ever see

Brown's drafting process is extremely wasteful by design.

He expects enormous amounts of writing to disappear.

The workbook says the final book should consist of the strongest distilled material from a much larger body of prose, dialogue, and plot. 

His 2025 comments give an astonishing example: for a roughly 200,000-word *Secret of Secrets*, he said he may have written around a million words, with hundreds of thousands discarded. ([NHPBS Video][6])

That's a useful psychological correction.

Deleted writing isn't necessarily wasted writing.

It may have been the material required to discover the book.

---

# Editing happens both during and after drafting

Brown distinguishes two forms of revision.

### Daily editing

Review recent work.

Tighten it.

Ask:

Does it make sense?

Does it accomplish what I intended?

Does it feel right?

### Manuscript editing

After finishing, step away.

Then return and experience the whole thing as much as possible like a reader.

He recommends printing it when possible because changing the medium can help alter your perspective. 

---

# Give the manuscript distance

After living inside a novel for months or years, you know things the reader doesn't.

You mentally fill gaps.

You overlook repetition.

You remember motivations the manuscript never actually explains.

Brown recommends letting the finished manuscript sit for weeks or even months before the major revision when possible. 

Distance restores some ignorance.

And ignorance is what readers bring to page one.

---

# Edit from the reader's perspective

This might be the overarching Brown revision principle.

Ask:

Where am I bored?

Where am I confused?

What am I expecting?

Has this become predictable?

Do I still care?

Was that promise fulfilled?

Did the reveal arrive too soon?

Do I need this fact?

Would I turn the page?

The manuscript isn't being graded for how much work you put into it.

It's being experienced by someone who owes it nothing.

---

# Don't abandon the core idea merely because execution is difficult

Brown distinguishes:

**The idea doesn't work**

from

**I haven't figured out how to make the idea work yet.**

His workbook warns that abandoning the original idea that truly excited you can sometimes be a form of lazy editing. Maybe the character, structure, opening, or approach needs to change rather than the central inspiration. 

That's a useful question during difficult revisions:

> **Is the premise broken—or is my current solution broken?**

Those aren't the same thing.

---

# Don't edit the life out of the book

Brown also warns against endlessly revising the same problem areas.

At some point revision becomes procrastination.

You can polish something until the spontaneity that made it interesting disappears.

The workbook advises balancing refinement with preservation of the novel's original energy. 

---

# Use systems to make revision visible

Brown has used a color system:

**Green:** satisfied.

**Yellow:** uncertain.

**Red:** needs work.

Then the goal becomes:

> Turn the manuscript green.

The workbook suggests this as a way to turn editing into a concrete, almost game-like process rather than an amorphous feeling that "the book isn't good enough." 

That's a surprisingly practical idea.

---

# Find readers who are objective enough to hurt your feelings a little

Brown wants outside readers who:

understand the kind of book you're trying to write,

but aren't emotionally obligated to praise you.

Other writers can be particularly useful because they understand craft problems. 

The goal isn't:

> Tell me it's good.

It's:

> **Tell me where the spell breaks.**

---

# Process matters more than inspiration

This becomes increasingly important in Brown's later teaching.

The workbook's process chapter says inspiration and craft are useless if you don't repeatedly sit down and work. 

Brown wants a **ritual**.

A place.

A time.

A routine.

Something the brain begins associating with:

> Now we write.

---

# Be tough on consistency, gentle on output

This is one of Brown's best productivity principles.

Don't necessarily demand:

> I must write 2,000 words today.

Demand:

> **I will show up and work today.**

The workbook specifically advises being forgiving about how many words you produce while remaining strict about maintaining the practice. 

That makes sense for a heavy outliner/reviser like Brown.

Some days are drafting days.

Some are restructuring.

Some are solving.

Some are cutting.

Word count alone doesn't capture the work.

---

# Write hours, not pages

That is essentially Brown's formulation.

Protect the writing period.

Let today's needs determine what happens inside it.

This continues to describe his real process. In a 2018 interview, Brown said he might spend a morning writing, outlining, or editing depending on what the book required. ([The Guardian][7])

---

# Remove distractions completely

Brown's attached workbook recommends a dedicated environment without:

email,

internet,

phone,

or other easy distractions.

And if he discovers a fact he needs while drafting, he may leave himself a note rather than immediately going online and breaking concentration. 

This is particularly relevant now.

You can lose twenty minutes researching the correct model of pistol and somehow end up reading about seventeenth-century naval warfare.

Mark it:

> [CHECK GUN]

Keep writing.

Research it later.

---

# Write extremely early if that's when the world leaves you alone

Brown's famous schedule is not a gimmick invented for MasterClass.

He has described it consistently for decades.

He gets up around **4 a.m.**, partly because there are fewer emails and distractions and partly because he likes moving from the dream state into writing as quickly as possible. ([The Guardian][7])

The lesson isn't:

**All serious writers wake at four.**

It's:

> **Find the period when nobody owns your attention except you.**

Then protect it.

---

# Write every day when you're carrying a complicated book

Brown's 2025 process makes this especially clear.

He told the Associated Press that he writes every day partly because his plots contain so many moving parts that after too many sleep cycles the details begin to evaporate from working memory. He also uses an elaborate visual board resembling a detective's case board to keep the novel organized. ([Yahoo][8])

Consistency isn't only discipline.

It maintains **story continuity inside the writer's mind**.

---

# "Set the table for breakfast"

This is a great Brown technique.

Before stopping for the day, leave tomorrow's self a note describing:

what happens next;

what problem you're solving;

what idea you're following;

or even a paragraph that leads into the next scene.

The workbook calls this **"setting the table for breakfast."** 

Instead of beginning tomorrow with:

> Where was I?

you begin with:

> Right. This.

It lowers the friction of starting.

---

# Allow terrible ideas

Brown recommends deliberate brainstorming sessions where bad ideas are welcome.

Why?

Because demanding that every idea be good creates paralysis.

The attached workbook says to give crazy ideas room because occasionally one becomes useful. 

This is creativity as volume:

**Bad idea.**

**Bad idea.**

**Ridiculous idea.**

**Interesting variation.**

**Wait...**

There it is.

---

# The cure for writer's block is writing

Brown's 2018 formulation is beautifully simple:

> Write something bad that nobody will ever see.

He says the act of writing often reveals the path back toward better material, and he estimates throwing out vastly more pages than he ultimately keeps. ([The Guardian][7])

That's completely consistent with his drafting philosophy.

Writer's block isn't solved by producing perfection.

It's solved by **producing material**.

---

# Change your physical state when your brain gets stuck

Brown famously exercises while writing.

His attached workbook recommends regular movement because physical activity can stimulate fresh ideas. 

His real routine includes hourly breaks for things such as push-ups, sit-ups, and stretching. He's also famously used gravity boots and later an inversion table. ([The Guardian][7])

The generalizable lesson is not:

> Hang upside down.

It's:

> **Don't assume every mental problem is solved by sitting motionless and staring harder.**

Walk.

Stretch.

Move.

Then return.

---

# Use rituals when self-doubt appears

Brown's expanded MasterClass material around *The Secret of Secrets* becomes unusually personal here.

He acknowledges that self-doubt remains part of novel writing even after enormous success. The workbook advises returning to your process and rituals when confidence collapses. 

For *The Secret of Secrets*, he even created a fake physical cover for the unfinished book and mocked up a bestseller list with the nonexistent book already at number one. He used them to convince himself that the finished book already existed and that his job was simply to reach it. ([MasterClass][9])

That's less a publishing strategy than a psychological one:

**Create tangible evidence of the future you're trying to build.**

---

# Respect the reader's intelligence

This has become an especially explicit part of Brown's recent advice.

In 2025 he said respecting the reader means not spoon-feeding everything and not wasting their time with material the book doesn't need. ([Audible.com][3])

The reader should have enough information to:

speculate,

solve,

predict,

and participate.

But you still control when certainty arrives.

---

# Respect the reader's time

This is the other half.

Brown is acutely aware that a novel is asking someone to surrender perhaps ten or twenty hours of their life.

His 2025 *Los Angeles Times* interview makes this responsibility explicit: once readers trust you enough to spend that much time with your work, you need to deliver. ([Los Angeles Times][1])

That explains many otherwise separate Brown rules:

short chapters;

transparent prose;

minimal unnecessary research;

constant promises;

aggressive editing;

clear stakes;

fast information exchange.

They all arise from:

> **Don't waste the reader's attention once they've given it to you.**

---

# Read critically

Brown says reading is essential, but eventually writers have to learn to read differently.

Don't only ask:

> Did I enjoy this?

Ask:

Why couldn't I stop reading?

Where did I become bored?

What information was withheld?

When did I predict the twist?

How did the author introduce the villain?

Why did that chapter ending work?

How did the writer make me care?

The workbook explicitly recommends studying both what excites you and what fails to excite you. 

Your own emotional reaction becomes craft data.

---

# Learn from everybody without trying to become them

Brown's course ultimately describes a **toolbox**.

The goal isn't:

> Write exactly like Dan Brown.

It's:

> Understand why these devices work, then use whichever ones serve your own voice.

His introductory note says the purpose is to help writers develop a story that is distinctly their own. 

That's important because Brown's most transferable skill isn't short chapters or historical conspiracies.

It's **understanding the reader's attention**.

# The Dan Brown Novel-Writing Checklist

If I reduced Brown's entire method into a working checklist for a novel, I'd use these questions:

1. **What subject am I desperate to know more about?**
2. **What "world" does that subject give me?**
3. **What two or three unrelated interests could I combine inside that world?**
4. **What morally gray question can reasonable people disagree about?**
5. **Can I turn that intellectual question into an immediate human problem?**
6. **What is my sole dramatic question?**
7. **What is the primary contract I'm making with the reader?**
8. **What is the answer to that question at the ending?**
9. **Do I know the villain, twist, and basic ending before I begin?**
10. **What does the world look like at the beginning versus the end?**
11. **What obstacles are necessary to force that transformation?**
12. **Can I summarize each major event on one index card?**
13. **Where does the middle become soft?**
14. **How can I make the protagonist's current problem worse?**
15. **What is the clock?**
16. **What is the crucible?**
17. **What prevents the protagonist from simply walking away?**
18. **What does the villain believe they're accomplishing?**
19. **Why does the villain believe their actions are morally justified?**
20. **What flaw makes the hero's journey harder?**
21. **What matters most personally to the hero?**
22. **What's the worst thing that could happen to them?**
23. **How can I make it worse?**
24. **Does every character have a clear purpose?**
25. **What does every important character want?**
26. **Could two secondary characters be combined?**
27. **Does the sidekick provide a skill or worldview the hero lacks?**
28. **Can disagreement between characters deepen the central moral question?**
29. **What does the reader know that the hero doesn't?**
30. **What does the hero know that the reader doesn't?**
31. **What am I plausibly withholding right now?**
32. **What question is driving this chapter?**
33. **When I answer that question, what new question replaces it?**
34. **Does every chapter create or fulfill a promise?**
35. **Can I compress the timeline?**
36. **Do I need more action—or more anticipation before the action?**
37. **Can a pulse remind the reader of danger during a quieter passage?**
38. **Where can I end immediately before the payoff?**
39. **Would switching POV after this cliffhanger increase tension?**
40. **Is my red herring fair and naturally generated by the story?**
41. **Will the eventual twist feel surprising but obvious in retrospect?**
42. **What single job must this chapter accomplish?**
43. **Does the reader learn something new here?**
44. **Do I have enough negative space between high-intensity scenes?**
45. **Am I varying tension and release like music?**
46. **Should this event be fully dramatized, or can I summarize it?**
47. **Am I staying consistently inside one POV per scene?**
48. **Have I researched enough to write confidently?**
49. **Have I talked to people who actually understand this world?**
50. **Can I visit the setting and experience it myself?**
51. **Am I using research because the reader needs it—or because I worked hard to learn it?**
52. **What 90 percent of the research can I leave out?**
53. **Is exposition happening through character, conflict, movement, or sensory experience?**
54. **Is my prose transparent enough that readers remain inside the story?**
55. **What words can I remove?**
56. **What does each character want during this conversation?**
57. **Can dialogue contain more disagreement?**
58. **Can characters move while delivering necessary information?**
59. **Have I written enough material to discover the best version instead of demanding perfection immediately?**
60. **What needs cutting even though I like it?**
61. **Have I given the manuscript enough distance before the major edit?**
62. **Am I experiencing the revision like a reader instead of like the person who wrote it?**
63. **Is the central idea failing—or does my current execution of it simply need changing?**
64. **Am I revising productively or merely tinkering because I'm afraid to finish?**
65. **Have I protected consistent writing time?**
66. **Am I measuring commitment by showing up rather than obsessing over daily word count?**
67. **Have I removed distractions during the writing period?**
68. **Did I leave tomorrow's self a clear starting point?**
69. **Am I allowing myself to generate bad ideas?**
70. **When I'm blocked, am I willing to write badly until movement returns?**
71. **Am I maintaining the physical and mental routine that lets me keep doing this?**
72. **Above everything else: what does the reader desperately want to know next?**

The deepest Brown principle underneath all of these is probably:

### A novel is a carefully managed sequence of promises.

You make the reader want something.

You delay it.

You make obtaining it harder.

You reveal part of it.

You create another question.

You force the protagonist deeper into the crucible.

You give the reader just enough information to form a theory.

You undermine that theory fairly.

You fulfill the smaller promise.

You replace it with a larger one.

And eventually you answer the **sole dramatic question** that has been driving the book from the beginning.

What I find especially interesting after comparing Brown's MasterClass with his 2025 interviews is that **his method has become, if anything, even more structural rather than less**. He is unequivocally a plotter: he wants the ending, villain, twist, and basic architecture before drafting. Yet he is simultaneously willing to generate vast amounts of disposable prose and change the path through the middle. So his real method isn't rigid outlining. It's closer to:

**Know the destination with extraordinary clarity. Build strong scaffolding. Then give yourself enormous creative freedom—and enormous permission to fail—while figuring out the most entertaining possible route between the beginning and the end.**

That also makes him a particularly useful complement to Baldacci. **Baldacci increasingly discovers his destination through characters; Brown knows the destination and uses characters, obstacles, information, and suspense to engineer the journey toward it.** Both are trying to produce page-turners, but they approach the architecture from nearly opposite directions.
