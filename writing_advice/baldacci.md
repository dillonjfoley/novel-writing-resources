I went through the attached *David Baldacci Teaches Mystery and Thriller Writing* workbook and compared it with his public MasterClass material, his official FAQ, interviews, and a particularly useful July 2026 *How I Write* interview in which he revisited many of the same ideas years later.

What stands out is that Baldacci's method is built around **curiosity, plausibility, character, reader engagement, compression, and momentum**. He is much less formula-driven than you might expect from someone who writes tightly engineered commercial thrillers. He plans enough to get moving, but he believes much of the real story is discovered while writing.

# David Baldacci's Method of Writing

## See the ordinary world through a "writer's prism"

This is probably the foundation of Baldacci's creative process.

He doesn't wait for gigantic story ideas. He observes normal life and asks what might really be happening underneath it.

A person boarding a bus with a package.

Two people talking outside a building.

Tourists walking near the White House.

Then imagination takes over:

What's in the package?

Who are those people really?

What if that innocent exchange is actually a signal?

The attached workbook calls this the **writer's prism**: take what the world gives you, filter it through your imagination, and transform it into something unexpected. 

In his 2026 interview, Baldacci says essentially the same thing decades into his career: he gets ideas simply by walking out the door, observing something, and asking unanswered questions about it. ([How I Write][1])

A useful Baldacci exercise is therefore:

> Look at something ordinary and ask, **"What if this isn't what it appears to be?"**

---

# Curiosity is one of a writer's greatest assets

Baldacci's official site says he's effectively always gathering story material—while traveling, walking dogs, sitting in meetings, or taking out the trash. His argument is that natural curiosity creates possible storylines everywhere. ([David Baldacci][2])

This isn't limited to mystery writers.

The writer's job is to notice:

what doesn't make sense,

what might be hidden,

what might happen next,

why somebody behaved strangely,

what two unrelated events might have in common.

Baldacci's imagination often begins with a **question rather than an answer**.

---

# Combine things that normally don't belong together

Baldacci recommends building a broad field of knowledge because originality often comes from connecting things other people wouldn't connect.

The workbook advises saving articles, interviews, observations, and other pieces of information that fascinate you. The broader your knowledge, the greater your chance of finding surprising combinations. 

His 2026 description calls these mental reservoirs **"knowledge pots."** Learn constantly. Then one day an idea draws from several different pots, producing a combination unique to you. ([How I Write][1])

That's a useful theory of originality:

**Originality doesn't necessarily require inventing from nothing.**

It can come from:

**Thing A + Thing B + Thing C + your perspective.**

---

# Write what interests you, not merely what you know

Baldacci repeatedly pushes back against "write what you know."

His preferred principle is closer to:

**Write what you want to know.**

The attached workbook says passion is the fuel that sustains a long project. You can write about something you already understand deeply, or something you know almost nothing about—as long as you're genuinely interested enough to learn. 

His official FAQ remains very direct about this: choose what interests you because writing is difficult enough that boredom makes quitting easy. ([David Baldacci][2])

That gives you a good project-selection question:

> **Would I willingly spend a year thinking about this subject?**

If not, the premise may not be enough.

---

# Don't chase trends

This follows naturally.

Baldacci warns against seeing somebody else's bestseller and deciding:

> That's selling. I'll write one of those.

The workbook says trends are only worth following when they overlap naturally with your genuine interests. 

His more recent comments are even stronger. In 2026 he says his first question is whether **he is excited by the idea**. If not, he shouldn't write it, regardless of whatever genre or trend is currently popular. ([How I Write][1])

Commercial awareness matters.

But enthusiasm has to come first.

---

# Let ideas percolate before committing

Baldacci is eager to start, but experience has taught him not to start *too* quickly.

The workbook recommends putting ideas aside for days or weeks and then returning to evaluate them. 

In his 2026 interview, he explains why: he's sometimes begun a novel too quickly and written himself into a wall. Now he gives promising ideas enough time to develop before committing to them. ([How I Write][1])

So there is a difference between:

**having an exciting idea**

and

**having an idea capable of supporting a novel.**

Give it a little pressure first.

---

# Find the problem that can sustain the book

Baldacci gravitates toward real-world problems with **moral complexity**.

The workbook explicitly warns against overly black-and-white situations. A stronger story may involve people doing questionable things for understandable reasons or attempting to achieve legitimate goals through troubling methods. 

This creates ambiguity.

The reader thinks:

> That's wrong.

Then:

> But I understand why they did it.

That tension can sustain much more story than:

> Hero good. Villain evil.

---

# Make morality complicated

Baldacci particularly likes characters and institutions existing in gray areas.

A government assassin may kill people—but believe he's protecting his country.

A law-enforcement officer may break rules to obtain justice.

A person may commit something terrible for a motive that began as something sympathetic.

Baldacci wants readers occasionally asking:

> **What would I do?**

That's more interesting than giving them an obvious moral answer.

---

# Start with the "Big Pop"

This is one of Baldacci's signature techniques.

The **Big Pop** is the compelling dramatic event, question, disturbance, or mystery that launches the novel.

His workbook says the first chapter needs something sufficiently unusual to make the reader want an answer. Readers don't have to understand everything—but they must understand enough not to feel lost. 

The key distinction is:

**Mystery = good.**

**Confusion = bad.**

You might begin with:

a murder,

an escape,

a disappearance,

a betrayal,

a strange discovery,

a disturbing conversation,

or simply something that appears impossible.

But something needs to make the reader think:

> **What the hell is going on?**

---

# The opening should generate the rest of the novel

Baldacci's 2026 explanation of the Big Pop goes deeper.

The opening shouldn't merely be exciting.

It should create **story possibilities**.

An inmate escapes an impossible prison.

Immediately:

Who helped him?

Why?

Where is he going?

Who wants him caught?

Why was he imprisoned?

What happens if he succeeds?

A good opening therefore acts almost like a seed containing the rest of the novel. Baldacci argues that if you plant enough necessary threads in the first few chapters, those threads tell you where to go next. ([How I Write][1])

This gives you a powerful opening test:

> **Does my first disturbance create enough questions to generate fifty more chapters?**

---

# Mislead the reader early

Baldacci has sharpened his Big Pop philosophy over time.

In his 2026 interview, he says one goal is to encourage experienced thriller readers to form an assumption early—and then prove that assumption wrong.

If the reader realizes:

> I thought I knew where this was going, and I was completely wrong,

they become less confident that they can predict the book. ([How I Write][1])

That's especially important for experienced mystery readers.

They've seen:

the suspicious husband,

the corrupt cop,

the missing witness,

the secret sibling,

the apparently dead villain.

So going only **one layer deeper** may not be enough.

Baldacci wants the writer thinking several layers beyond the obvious explanation.

---

# But never cheat

Misdirection isn't lying to the reader.

The workbook's technique is:

1. Raise an unresolved question.
2. Suggest a plausible interpretation.
3. Allow the character and reader to believe it.
4. Eventually reveal that the assumption was wrong.
5. Show the consequences of that mistaken belief. 

The crucial point is that the false conclusion should arise naturally.

You give the reader **real evidence that they misinterpret**, rather than withholding something solely because the author needs a twist.

---

# A mystery is a house of cards

Baldacci emphasizes that clues must connect.

If you introduce something early, it needs to matter later.

His MasterClass explanation describes mystery construction almost like a house of cards: clues, red herrings, deception, and twists depend upon one another, and an unsupported piece can weaken the whole construction. ([MasterClass][3])

That means every mystery clue creates a promise.

Eventually you need to satisfy it.

---

# Build mysteries one clue at a time

At the same time, Baldacci warns against trying to solve the whole 400-page puzzle before writing page one.

That's overwhelming.

Instead:

**one clue → another clue → another complication → another discovery.**

His advice is to take "baby steps" and allow the mystery architecture to emerge incrementally. ([MasterClass][3])

This is a recurring Baldacci principle:

### Make the big thing small enough to work on today.

---

# Outlines are tools, not commandments

The attached workbook presents Baldacci as something of a middle-ground outliner.

He may use:

bullet points,

broad story movements,

mini-outlines,

partial chapter plans,

or something more elaborate.

But he explicitly says outlines should evolve. If something better happens during drafting, follow it. 

This is important:

**The outline serves the novel.**

The novel does not serve the outline.

---

# His process has become even more discovery-based

This is one area where his later comments are particularly interesting.

In his 2021 PBS interview, Baldacci said he virtually never knows how a novel will end when he begins and often discovers the real story only after he's deeply engaged with the characters. ([PBS][4])

In his July 2026 interview, he went even further: lengthy outlines no longer work for him. He prefers writing linearly, page one forward, making important decisions when he's actually immersed in the scene. ([How I Write][1])

So his current method seems roughly:

**Think → percolate → establish a strong opening → know a few broad movements → start writing → discover → periodically reassess.**

Not pure outlining.

Not blind improvisation.

---

# Don't figure out the whole novel at once

Baldacci compares attempting a novel all at once to overwhelming yourself with an impossibly large problem.

Instead, break it apart.

Write a couple chapters.

Then another couple.

Learn the characters.

Discover the next problem.

Eventually the shape begins to emerge.

In 2026, he described a novel as almost becoming **a sequence of connected short stories**. ([How I Write][1])

That's a useful psychological trick for anyone intimidated by an 80,000-word manuscript.

Don't write:

> My novel.

Write:

> **This scene. Then this chapter. Then the next problem.**

---

# Character matters more than plot

This is especially interesting coming from a bestselling thriller writer.

In 2026 Baldacci was asked directly whether he valued character or plot more.

His answer was character.

A brilliant plot populated by people nobody cares about ultimately fails because the reader has no emotional reason to care whether anyone survives. ([How I Write][1])

So:

**Plot makes readers ask what happens next.**

**Character makes them care about the answer.**

You need both.

But character gives the plot emotional meaning.

---

# Give characters baggage

One of Baldacci's favorite concepts is **baggage**.

Characters shouldn't enter the book as clean machines built solely to solve this particular plot.

They already had lives.

Failures.

Families.

Regrets.

Trauma.

Guilt.

Ambitions.

Relationships.

Mistakes.

In his 2026 interview, Baldacci explains that recurring characters especially need emotional material outside the immediate mystery, because that baggage gives them something to reveal and evolve through over multiple books. ([How I Write][1])

Even in a standalone novel, the principle works.

Ask:

> **What problem did this character have before page one that the plot doesn't magically erase?**

---

# Know what every character wants

Baldacci's workbook advises determining motivation for **everyone**, including the villain.

What does this person want?

Why?

Why does it matter enough to produce their behavior?

And are those desires believable?



That's particularly important when the character does something extreme.

---

# Match the size of the motivation to the size of the action

Baldacci articulated this particularly clearly in 2026.

If a character commits an extraordinary act, the underlying motivation must be powerful enough to justify it psychologically.

Someone doesn't murder six people because:

> They were having a bad week.

The magnitude of the motive needs to correspond to the magnitude of the behavior. ([How I Write][1])

That's an excellent diagnostic test for villains.

If the act feels implausible, the problem may not be the act.

The **motivation may be too small**.

---

# Your villain thinks they're right

Baldacci rejects villains who function only as evil machinery.

His workbook argues that people generally act according to what **they** consider justified. Therefore, the writer must understand the antagonist's belief system as thoroughly as the protagonist's. 

Ask your villain:

> Why are you doing this?

Then keep asking:

> Why?

until the answer is psychologically convincing.

The villain doesn't have to be correct.

But their behavior has to make sense **to them**.

---

# Push characters beyond their limits

Baldacci recommends identifying your character's nadir:

> What's the worst thing that could happen to them?

Then his workbook exercise goes one step farther:

> **How can I make it even worse?**



His 2026 version is even more vivid: he doesn't merely want characters pushed against a wall—he wants to push them **through** it and see what comes out the other side. ([How I Write][1])

Why?

Because easy circumstances don't reveal much.

Pressure reveals character.

---

# Give characters difficult choices with consequences

Character isn't only revealed by biography.

It is revealed through **decisions under pressure**.

Baldacci says stories need:

conflict,

decisions,

stakes,

and consequences.

Force someone to choose between two things they value.

Loyalty versus justice.

Family versus duty.

Safety versus truth.

Career versus conscience.

Then make the choice cost something. ([How I Write][1])

A decision without consequences isn't much of a decision.

---

# Let characters change

A character shouldn't simply complete the external plot and emerge psychologically untouched.

Baldacci wants an arc.

Maybe they are:

more damaged,

more healed,

more cynical,

more hopeful,

more capable,

less certain,

redeemed,

or destroyed.

But something should happen internally because of what they endured. ([How I Write][1])

The reader should be able to look at the beginning and ending and think:

> **I understand how this person became that person.**

---

# First appearances matter

Baldacci urges writers to pay close attention to the first time readers meet important characters.

Instead of simply describing a trait, **demonstrate it**.

Don't say:

> Marcus was brilliant with numbers.

Have Marcus instantly calculate something nobody else in the room notices.

The workbook also recommends letting characterization accumulate gradually rather than delivering an introductory dossier all at once. 

Characterization should happen while the story is happening.

---

# Give secondary characters memorable distinctions

Baldacci doesn't want minor characters to blur together.

A secondary character might have:

a peculiar article of clothing,

a verbal habit,

a memorable name,

a physical trait,

an unusual interest,

or a distinctive previous encounter.

Just enough that when they return fifty pages later, the reader thinks:

> Oh, *that* person.



That's reader courtesy.

---

# Don't treat secondary characters as furniture

His recent advice goes further.

Even peripheral characters deserve some depth.

They may have fewer pages, but they should still feel as though they possess lives beyond the function they're performing for the plot. Baldacci argues that developing the supporting cast gives the entire novel a richer feeling. ([How I Write][1])

A receptionist isn't merely:

> Receptionist who gives hero information.

She can still be a human being.

---

# Every scene should have a purpose—preferably more than one

This is one of Baldacci's most useful lessons.

His screenplay experience taught him that a scene ideally accomplishes at least **two things**.

Perhaps it:

advances the investigation **and** reveals the detective's guilt;

introduces the villain **and** establishes the setting;

delivers information **and** changes a relationship.

His workbook includes an exercise asking writers to identify every scene's goals and see whether scenes can multitask. 

In 2026 he phrased the broader rule this way: if a scene or even a line doesn't advance plot, deepen character, or provide necessary information, question why it's there. ([How I Write][1])

---

# Compress

Compression may be the most repeated technical principle in Baldacci's teaching.

Get more story out of fewer words.

More character out of fewer beats.

More information out of fewer scenes.

The goal isn't necessarily short novels.

It's **dense storytelling**.

Every element earns its space.

---

# Give the reader slightly less information than they want

Baldacci says one of the great advantages of suspense writing is controlling information.

His workbook recommends telling readers what they need—but ideally slightly less than they want.

That gap creates curiosity. 

The reader keeps turning pages because they are always pursuing the missing piece.

This is the information equivalent of leaving a door slightly open.

---

# Every reveal has timing

Information isn't merely:

**revealed / not revealed.**

There is also:

**when**.

Baldacci's recent discussion calls this **micro-timing**.

A revelation one page earlier or later can dramatically change suspense because it alters what the reader knows during everything surrounding it. ([How I Write][1])

During revision, don't merely ask:

> Should the reader know this?

Ask:

> **What is the most powerful moment for the reader to know this?**

---

# Think of pacing as a roller coaster

Baldacci does not believe thrillers should run at maximum speed continuously.

That exhausts readers.

His workbook describes pacing as a **roller coaster**:

build slowly,

rise,

increase anticipation,

drop into the thrill,

recover,

then begin climbing again.



Slow scenes aren't automatically bad.

They become bad when nothing interesting is happening.

---

# Escalate the thrills

The major suspense moments should generally become more consequential as the novel continues.

If the most exciting event happens on page 60 and everything afterward feels smaller, momentum collapses.

The workbook advises spreading major moments throughout the story while escalating their stakes toward the end. 

Think:

**trouble → bigger trouble → disaster → catastrophe.**

---

# Cliffhangers don't need people literally hanging from cliffs

A cliffhanger simply withholds resolution at the moment curiosity is highest.

The gun fires.

Chapter ends.

The character opens the envelope.

Chapter ends.

Someone says:

> "There's something you need to know."

Chapter ends.

The workbook notes that many thriller writers end chapters this way, sometimes even switching characters afterward before eventually returning to the unresolved question. 

The technique works because the reader already invested attention into getting the answer.

---

# Create a new question before answering the old one

That's the deeper trick behind page-turning fiction.

Resolve something.

But before the resolution gives the reader permission to stop, create another uncertainty.

The resulting chain becomes:

**Question → partial answer → bigger question → revelation → new danger → another question.**

The reader keeps borrowing another chapter.

---

# Don't rely on twists alone

This is an important qualification from Baldacci's recent thinking.

Twists, red herrings, and misdirection are part of his genre.

But a book cannot merely be:

> Surprise! You guessed wrong!

He wants readers to remember **what happened to the characters**, not merely who the murderer was. ([How I Write][1])

So:

**Plot twist + emotional consequence**

is much stronger than:

**plot twist alone.**

---

# Suspense is anticipation, not merely action

Baldacci loves action, but he understands that the anticipation of something happening is frequently more powerful than the thing itself.

His pacing method deliberately lets the reader sit near the top of the roller coaster before releasing them. ([How I Write][1])

You don't need:

gunfight → explosion → chase → murder → explosion.

Sometimes:

**There is someone upstairs.**

is enough.

Especially if you make the reader wait.

---

# Slow down your biggest moments

This is one of the most interesting pieces of his 2026 advice.

Writers often instinctively write action faster:

short sentences,

quick beats,

rapid movement.

Baldacci argues that the truly consequential moments can become more powerful when you **slow them down**.

Let the character notice.

Let consequences register.

Let the reader experience what happened.

He cites *Unforgiven* as an influence because violence doesn't merely occur and disappear—the audience has to remain with its human consequences. ([How I Write][1])

The principle is:

> **The more important the moment, the less eager you should be to escape it.**

---

# Action should reveal character

Baldacci doesn't view action as a break from characterization.

Action is characterization.

How someone behaves under threat tells us:

whether they're courageous,

reckless,

trained,

selfish,

compassionate,

resourceful,

or terrified.

The workbook specifically recommends using action to reveal abilities and personality rather than merely creating spectacle. 

A chase shouldn't only answer:

> Who gets away?

It can also answer:

> **Who is this person when everything goes wrong?**

---

# Violence needs consequences

People aren't action figures.

If someone gets beaten, shot, stabbed, concussed, exhausted, or terrified, that should have some effect.

Baldacci warns against cartoonish action where characters absorb tremendous physical punishment and casually continue. 

Consequences add credibility.

And often emotional weight.

---

# Dialogue has to know what scene it's in

Before writing dialogue, Baldacci wants two things clear:

**What is the scene supposed to accomplish?**

and

**What is everyone feeling right now?**

The workbook calls this understanding the scene's **emotional sequence**. 

If two characters nearly killed each other in the previous chapter, they shouldn't suddenly exchange calm exposition because the author needs information conveyed.

Emotional continuity matters.

---

# Dialogue should be brief

Baldacci's rule of thumb is **brevity**.

In his workbook he recommends:

sentence fragments,

cutting filler,

eliminating repetition,

avoiding unnecessary names,

avoiding speeches,

and letting physical behavior convey emotion. 

His 2026 comments are even harsher: long dialogue can become lazy because writers use speech to explain things they could communicate more efficiently through action, narration, or carefully chosen details. ([How I Write][1])

The useful test is:

> **Can ten lines become one great line?**

---

# Dialogue must either move story or reveal character

Characters in novels don't have the luxury of endlessly chatting the way people do in reality.

Baldacci wants dialogue to earn its place.

It can:

advance the plot,

reveal emotion,

expose conflict,

provide information,

show power relationships,

or deepen characterization.

Preferably several at once.

Otherwise, cut it.

---

# Read dialogue aloud

Baldacci recommends listening to real people and physically reading dialogue aloud during revision.

Your eye may accept a sentence your mouth instantly exposes as false.



If you can't comfortably hear the character saying it, the reader probably won't either.

---

# Become a student of humanity

This may be his broadest dialogue advice.

Listen.

Watch people.

Pay attention to:

interruptions,

silences,

facial reactions,

hesitations,

body language,

and what people don't say.

An older *Washington Post* interview has Baldacci explaining that some of the most revealing communication isn't verbal at all. ([The Washington Post][5])

Real dialogue isn't simply:

Person A speaks.

Person B responds.

Person A answers.

Human beings evade, misunderstand, lie, interrupt, gesture, and conceal.

Put that on the page.

---

# Research like a journalist

For realism, Baldacci says the novelist sometimes needs to put away the novelist's hat and become a reporter.

Go to the place.

Talk to people.

Ask questions.

Observe.

Take notes.

Verify.

The workbook particularly emphasizes visiting locations and interviewing people with first-hand expertise. 

His MasterClass makes the same point: complicated worlds like the military or Secret Service require enough reality that readers can believe them. ([MasterClass][6])

---

# Research the emotional reality too

Facts aren't enough.

Baldacci's 2026 interview makes a useful distinction between what he calls **hardware** and **software** research.

Hardware is:

equipment,

procedures,

locations,

training,

technology.

Software is:

Why would somebody choose this life?

What does the job do to them?

What are they frightened of?

What does it feel like?

That second category often produces stronger characters. ([How I Write][1])

A convincing soldier is not created merely by knowing the correct rifle.

---

# Go there when you can

Baldacci strongly prefers firsthand experience because another person's account is filtered through **their** attention.

You may notice something completely different.

The workbook says visiting places often generates story ideas in addition to factual accuracy. 

In 2026, he explained that everyone looking at the same place notices different things, which is why he likes conducting as much of his own field research as possible. ([How I Write][1])

That's the writer's prism applied to research.

---

# Treat settings like characters

Locations have:

moods,

limitations,

histories,

textures,

sounds,

and practical consequences.

The workbook encourages researching settings deeply enough that the place can actually affect the plot. 

The environment shouldn't merely sit behind the characters.

It can create obstacles.

Opportunities.

Atmosphere.

Secrets.

---

# Plausibility matters more than probability

This is one of Baldacci's clearest fiction rules.

A story does not have to depict what **usually happens**.

It needs to depict something the reader believes **could happen**.

His workbook explicitly says the novelist is ultimately bound by plausibility rather than probability. 

That's enormously freeing.

Especially for thrillers, fantasy, science fiction, and adventure.

The extraordinary is allowed.

The unbelievable isn't.

---

# Don't let research imprison imagination

Research establishes credibility.

It shouldn't become a cage.

Baldacci warns that learning how things normally work can make writers afraid to imagine something unusual.

His response:

> If the unusual thing is plausibly possible inside the story, use it.



Reality gives you the foundation.

Fiction lets you build the unlikely thing on top.

---

# Leave almost all the research out

This may be Baldacci's strongest research rule.

You did the research so **you** understand the world.

The reader does not need your dissertation.

The workbook advises using only enough information to maintain credibility and curiosity. 

His 2026 version is even more extreme: he says something like 99% of research shouldn't end up on the page. ([How I Write][1])

Research is scaffolding.

The reader should see the building.

Not all the scaffolding.

---

# Details must earn their place

Baldacci warns against what could be called **research ego**:

> I spent three days learning this, so I'm putting three pages about it into the book.

No.

A detail deserves space because it:

creates atmosphere,

affects the plot,

reveals character,

adds credibility,

or produces emotion.

Not because researching it was difficult.

His recent advice is ruthless: everything has to prove its right to exist in the story. ([How I Write][1])

---

# Don't research instead of writing

Research is productive enough to feel virtuous and open-ended enough to become procrastination.

Baldacci says start writing once you know enough to create scenes. Continue researching while drafting. 

You do **not** need to know everything before page one.

You'll discover what you actually need to know by writing.

---

# Write wherever you can

Baldacci wrote while working full-time as a lawyer.

His official site recalls him writing late at night and in the early morning whenever time existed. ([David Baldacci][2])

He isn't particularly romantic about the perfect writing environment.

A Gotham Writers interview has him saying the ideal place to write is essentially **inside your head**. Don't let the perfect desk, pen, schedule, room, or ritual become an excuse. ([Gotham Writers Workshop][7])

That's consistent with the way he still works.

He writes in hotel rooms, on trips, and wherever ideas demand attention.

---

# Don't worship word counts

Baldacci doesn't use a rigid words-per-day target.

His reasoning is practical.

Two thousand bad words aren't inherently better than five hundred great ones.

And if you're in a productive creative flow, why stop merely because today's quota has been reached?

His 2026 comments again emphasize working according to the energy of the story rather than an arbitrary number. ([How I Write][1])

The important thing is not:

**Did I hit 2,000?**

It's:

**Am I moving the story forward?**

---

# Writing is a lifestyle, not merely a scheduled activity

Baldacci repeatedly describes himself as mentally writing even when he's nowhere near a keyboard.

Plotting.

Observing.

Imagining.

Solving.

Connecting.

His MasterClass describes the writer's prism as something incorporated into daily life rather than switched on only when sitting at a desk. ([MasterClass][8])

That's why his productivity isn't solely a matter of typing speed.

Much of the work happened before his fingers touched the keys.

---

# When blocked, leave the problem—but not writing

Baldacci has a practical writer's-block strategy.

Work on another project.

Take a walk.

Take a shower.

Daydream.

Let your conscious attention move somewhere else while your subconscious continues working on the problem. ([Gotham Writers Workshop][7])

His 2026 interview confirms this is still how he works: when he's too close to an ending or plot problem, stepping away often allows the solution to appear. ([How I Write][1])

The important distinction is:

**Step away from forcing the answer.**

Not:

**give up on the book.**

---

# Write the ending you have, then improve it

Baldacci sometimes uses what he calls a provisional or "pen" ending when the perfect conclusion hasn't arrived yet.

He writes a plausible version, gets distance from it, considers alternatives, and lets his subconscious continue solving the problem. ([How I Write][1])

That's useful beyond thrillers.

Don't freeze because you haven't discovered the brilliant ending.

Write **an ending**.

Then give yourself something tangible to improve.

---

# Edit ruthlessly

Baldacci enjoys revision.

His attached workbook describes two overlapping processes.

He revises continually during drafting, particularly returning to early chapters.

Then, after finishing, he prints the entire manuscript and performs a much more aggressive structural edit—moving chapters, deleting scenes, adding material, and deepening characters. 

He calls this the stage where the manuscript starts becoming the novel.

---

# Delete before you add

Baldacci believes first drafts are frequently overwritten.

In his 2026 account, much of the heavy lifting after reaching the end involves deciding:

what doesn't work,

what is misplaced,

what needs adding,

and particularly **what should disappear**. ([How I Write][1])

That's an important revision mindset.

Don't assume every problem requires another scene.

Sometimes the solution is subtraction.

---

# Cut at least ten percent

The workbook suggests that writers can often remove roughly ten percent simply through sentence-level tightening. 

Cut:

repetition,

filler,

unnecessary explanation,

redundant dialogue,

overlong description,

things the reader already understands.

Compression creates speed without requiring explosions.

---

# Leave your ego outside the manuscript

Research you love may need cutting.

A brilliant sentence may need cutting.

A character you adore may need cutting.

An action scene you spent two days writing may need cutting.

Baldacci's workbook specifically warns against becoming too precious about any element during the big revision. 

The question is not:

> Do I love this?

It's:

> **Does the novel need this?**

---

# Study the finished book like game film

One of the better additions from his 2026 interview is that Baldacci analyzes his own finished books afterward.

What worked?

What did readers predict too early?

Where did the twist telegraph itself?

Where did character development land?

If readers repeatedly interpreted something differently from how he intended, he treats that as evidence of a problem in the writing—not a failure of the reader. ([How I Write][1])

That is a useful professional mindset.

Finished doesn't mean nothing more can be learned from it.

---

# Approach every book as though you haven't mastered writing

Baldacci was once advised to approach every novel as if it were his first.

He still likes that idea.

Gotham Writers quotes him saying that a degree of fear keeps writers from becoming complacent; believing you've completely figured out the craft can dull the edge that made the work good. ([Gotham Writers Workshop][7])

His recent formulation is similar:

the more he knows what good writing can accomplish, the harder he pushes himself.

Mastery doesn't necessarily make the work easier.

It raises the standard.

---

# Read great fiction—not only books about writing

Baldacci believes strongly in learning craft by reading writers who already do it brilliantly.

His 2026 advice is to experience great novels first as a reader.

Then go back and ask:

Why did that affect me?

Where did the character change?

Where was the information revealed?

Why couldn't I stop reading?

What made this scene memorable?

([How I Write][1])

In other words:

**Experience first. Diagnose second.**

Great fiction is itself a craft textbook.

---

# Persevere through rejection

Baldacci wrote for years before *Absolute Power* became his first major sale.

The workbook treats rejection as a permanent part of a writing career rather than a hurdle that disappears after success.

His advice is to distinguish between:

**rejection as verdict**

and

**rejection as information.**

If useful criticism appears, learn from it.

But don't allow another person's rejection to determine whether you continue writing. 

---

# Don't become a copy of David Baldacci

The workbook begins with an important disclaimer.

His techniques are tools.

Take the ones that help.

Ignore the ones that don't.

Learn from other writers while developing your own interests and methods. 

That's consistent with his changing attitude toward outlining.

He doesn't believe there is one correct process.

There is only:

> **the process that helps you write this book.**

# The David Baldacci Novel-Writing Checklist

If I reduced his method to a working checklist, I'd use this:

1. **What am I genuinely fascinated by?**
2. **What ordinary thing can I look at through my writer's prism?**
3. **What unanswered question does the idea create?**
4. **What unrelated interests or knowledge can I combine with it?**
5. **Does the premise contain enough moral complexity for a novel?**
6. **Have I let the idea percolate long enough to know I want to commit?**
7. **What is my Big Pop?**
8. **Does the opening create several future story questions?**
9. **Does the reader understand enough to be curious rather than confused?**
10. **What obvious assumption will an experienced reader make—and can I plausibly undermine it?**
11. **What clues am I planting, and what eventual promises do they create?**
12. **Am I planning enough to move forward without strangling discovery?**
13. **What baggage does my protagonist bring into page one?**
14. **What does every important character want?**
15. **Is the motivation powerful enough to justify the behavior?**
16. **Why does my villain believe they're justified?**
17. **What's the worst thing that can happen to my protagonist?**
18. **How can I make it worse?**
19. **What difficult choices will reveal who the character really is?**
20. **How will the character be changed by the end?**
21. **Do secondary characters feel distinct and alive?**
22. **What are the purposes of this scene?**
23. **Can this scene accomplish two things instead of one?**
24. **Am I giving the reader slightly less information than they want?**
25. **Am I revealing this information at the most powerful possible moment?**
26. **Does the pacing rise and fall like a roller coaster?**
27. **Are the major suspense beats escalating?**
28. **Can this chapter end with an unresolved question?**
29. **Does the twist have emotional consequences, or is it merely clever?**
30. **Am I slowing down the scenes that deserve maximum emotional weight?**
31. **Does action reveal character and have realistic consequences?**
32. **What is the emotional sequence of this dialogue scene?**
33. **Can ten lines of dialogue become five—or one?**
34. **Does every line either move story, reveal character, or provide necessary information?**
35. **Does the dialogue sound believable aloud?**
36. **Have I researched the factual world enough to make it credible?**
37. **Have I researched what living in that world actually feels like?**
38. **Am I using only the tiny percentage of research the reader needs?**
39. **Is something technically unlikely but still plausibly convincing?**
40. **Am I researching because I need information—or because I'm avoiding writing?**
41. **Am I trying to solve the whole novel rather than the next few pages?**
42. **If I'm blocked, can I give the problem to my subconscious and work on something else?**
43. **What can I cut?**
44. **What can I say more powerfully in fewer words?**
45. **Have I looked at the completed book from the reader's perspective?**
46. **What did this book teach me that I can use on the next one?**
47. **Am I still excited by the story?**

The deepest Baldacci principle underneath all of these is probably:

### Give the reader reasons to keep asking questions—but give them characters worth caring about while they wait for the answers.

His thrillers aren't built solely around speed. They're built around a cycle:

**Observe something → ask a question → create a disturbance → give it to a damaged, motivated character → withhold part of the answer → complicate the moral situation → force decisions → escalate consequences → reveal information at exactly the right moment → change the character → cut everything that weakens the effect.**

And his recent 2026 interview makes one aspect of his philosophy even clearer than the older MasterClass does: **Baldacci may be famous for elaborate plots, but he increasingly talks like a character-first discovery writer.** Plot is essential, twists are essential, research is essential—but fascinating human beings under severe pressure are what make him want to keep writing, and what he believes ultimately makes readers care. ([How I Write][1])
